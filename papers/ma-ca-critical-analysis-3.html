Critical Analysis 3: Complaints and Blame in Troubles-Telling

Abstract

How a complaint about a faulty umbrella escalates from blaming a shop to blaming a person: a conversation-analytic look at troubles-telling, affiliative responses, embodied actions and the shift from situation-oriented to third-party-oriented complaints.

In this troubles-telling, Olivia is unfolding the events from lines 1-12. Olivia is telling a story, in form of a pre-complaint, of walking down the street in the rain, the use of oh my and the pause before and after is to emphasise her complaint in line 4 (Barraja-Rohan, 2003). Rob shows a display-of-interest by acknowledging her turn with a vocal continuer mm and embodied continuer of nodding (Pavlidou et al., 2022). Olivia then gives an account of the actual complaint that her umbrella was broken, emphasizing that it is a new one. Complaining is considered a form of social action, in which Olivia expresses dissatisfaction about buying a faulty umbrella (Drew and Walter, 2009). Rob responds with a display-of-concern and affiliation, and asks a follow-up question, showing display-of-interest (Drew and Walker, 2009). Olivia denies responsibility and shows disbelief about the shop selling her a faulty umbrella (lines 6 and 8), an example of a complaint-implicative in form of assigning blame, a complainable, an affective stance (Dersley & Wootton, 2000). Sasha interrupts Olivia with a pre-initial turn by pointing (line 7), which is an example of using embodied resources accepting the complaint by Olivia and assigning blame to the shop where Olivia bought the umbrella (Mondada, 2007). Sasha then verbally asks for clarification from Olivia about the shop (line 9), seeking confirmation (Pietikäinen, 2018). Rob looks at the shop (line 10), using embodied resources to show interest and engagement in the conversation, and pursues confirmation based on the question asked by Sasha (Pavlidou et al., 2022). Olivia confirms displaying affiliation, that the shop is the one in question, with a monosyllabic mm and embodied action by nodding three times (Pavlidou et al., 2022). Olivia (line 12) verbally confirms by accusing and emphasizing that the shop is dodgy, which is an example of using evaluative language to convey a negative assessment of the shop and charging fault, which is a complaint-implicative in form of a complainable and affective stance (Dersley & Wootton, 2000).

Sasha uses affiliation and is seeking sympathy by complaining about another faulty product from the same shop (Rodriguez, 2022). The silence in line 15 shows a change in the social action between the participants, as the accusation shifts from the shop, a situation-oriented complaint, to Sasha, a third-party-oriented complaint (Rodriguez, 2022). Rob seeks clarification (line 16) by questioning if the laptop was faulty or if she broke it herself by dropping it, showing his awareness of a wrongdoing as his hesitation and perturbation display past-troubles-talk (Barraja-Rohan, 2003). Olivia pursues this clarification request in an overlap tag question to what Rob asked, questioning the moral order of Sasha with a negative stance (Rodriguez, 2022). This is a form of criticism by declaring wrongdoing because breaking her laptop is not the same as Olivia buying a faulty umbrella (Pietikäinen, 2018). Sasha responds with well you know, which is a defensive account, then shifts blame by shifting the topic to past wrongdoing (Drew and Walter, 2009). After the inter-turn and turn-completion, there is an intra-turn silence, then she gives an alternative account, using it as non-complainable (Mondana, 2007). The silence shows that she is not getting a response, so she escalates in line 23, which is common denial responses (Drew and Walter, 2009).
