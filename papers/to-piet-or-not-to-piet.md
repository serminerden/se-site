# To Piet or Not to Piet

*First written in 2015, as a student at the Hogeschool van Amsterdam. This is the version I would write today — with the freedom I didn't have then, and with a decade of the debate behind us.*

---

For decades, families have stood waiting in torrential rain for the arrival of Sinterklaas — Saint Nicholas — and his helpers, the *Zwarte Pieten*, the Black Peters. I know that scene from the inside: the cold hands, the songs, the anticipation, and the figure on the white horse surrounded by grown men in blackface, afro wigs and jester's clothes. As a child you accept it as magic. As an adult who has lived between cultures, you eventually ask what — and who — that magic is built on.

The servant was never part of the oldest tradition. He was introduced by Jan Schenkman in his 1850 book *Sint Nicolaas en Zijn Knecht* (*Saint Nicholas and his Servant*): a single black-skinned servant, a product of the colonial imagination of his time, doing the menial work while the saint did the judging. And judge he did — the book reflected the stern didactics of its age. Sinterklaas would interrogate children on their behaviour and their religious catechisms; the good were rewarded with treats and presents, the bad were punished with a rod, or worse, threatened with being carried back to Spain in the servant's large burlap sack. The multiplication of the servants — from one Pietservant to a whole troop of them — came later, apparently after World War II, when Canadian soldiers organised the first post-liberation Sinterklaas celebrations and logistical needs gave us an army of helpers. A helper invented to carry the saint's burden became, over a century, a character children adored: silly, joyful, generous with sweets.

That is precisely why the debate is so painful. In recent years this beloved holiday has split the country in two. Opponents say the blackened face is racist and intolerant, a caricature inherited from a colonial past that has no business surviving in a multicultural society. Proponents are genuinely bewildered: how can a celebration be racist when it is *celebrated* — when Zwarte Piet is loved, not mocked? Both sides speak past each other, and the chasm between them has widened precisely because of cultural distance: a Dutch majority that experiences the tradition as innocent, and ethnic minorities who experience its imagery as a reminder that they are not fully at home in their own country.

What I understand now, and could not say then, is that neither side is simply right or simply wrong — but the asymmetry between them matters. The proponents have a point when they note that Zwarte Piet has been promoted over the decades from menial servant to Sinterklaas' equal partner; in their eyes he has moved forward, been embraced rather than stigmatised. But perception of the celebrator is not the same as perception of the celebrated. Research on racial bias in empathy makes this uncomfortably concrete: when researchers measured participants' physiological arousal, Caucasian observers reacted significantly less to pain suffered by African people than to pain suffered by Caucasians (Avenanti, Minio-Paluello, Bufalari & Aglioti, 2008; Avenanti, Sirigu & Aglioti, 2010). A deeply rooted, reflexive asymmetry in whose pain we register helps explain why one group's discomfort can remain genuinely invisible to the other. And those who *do* see it often arrived there through contact: the playwright Mark Walraven, who performed as a black-faced Zwarte Piet, stopped doing so after he began working with black colleagues — "many people are offended by this symbol" (Lindsey, 2008).

Meanwhile, the two groups await the same December evening with opposite feelings: one side anticipation and joy, the other apprehension, frustration and humiliation. The most vulnerable minorities are those who believe they are being discriminated against — and perception of group discrimination breeds tension and a weak sense of belonging. Instead of identifying with the nation-state, discriminated minorities tighten cohesion around their own ethnic group and turn inward, which only deepens the divide. Ethnic diversity can reach harmony only when minority and majority are bound together by a common sense of national unity (Rath, 2014). The imagery matters here: blackened faces, afro wigs and jester's clothes, worn almost always by white performers, carry a harsh undertone of humiliation for those who are told, in effect, that this is their place in the national pageant.

And yet the minority's original request was strikingly modest. Nobody was asking for the end of Sinterklaas. The ask was this: let *Zwarte Piet* simply become *Piet* — any colour, no blackface, the same silly, joyful, sweet-throwing character. Children keep their presents and their sweets; what disappears is only the racial connotation. It is a small edit to a tradition, not an amputation of it — and one step towards a national unity that both groups claim to want.

Why, then, the resistance? Because change, even small change, threatens the sense of safety. Human beings are creatures of habit; faced with too much change at once we instinctively retreat to the known, "safe" path rather than risk the unknown (Kanter, 2012). So the proponents defend the tradition to feel safe in what they know, and the opponents push for change to feel safe as equals in the society they live in — and the chasm grows. What broke the deadlock, interestingly, was not an argument but a mirror: after the documentary *Zwart als Roet* (*Black as Soot*, Bergman, 2014), in which an ethnic Dutch filmmaker confronts her own society's blind spots, many people crossed from the proponents' side to the opponents'. It showed that what was at stake was never really one character's make-up, but how intolerant Dutch society had become as a whole — and how both groups had forgotten the main idea behind the holiday: making our children happy.

Because that is the final irony. This argument divided a country in a way nobody intended, while both sides claimed the same goal. Since I first wrote this essay in 2015, the country has, in fact, moved: in the years that followed, city after city and broadcaster after broadcaster replaced the blackface with a soot-smudged Piet, and by the end of that decade the soot-smudged Piet had become the norm on Dutch screens. The prediction that Sinterklaas would die without his black paint proved false. The children adapted far faster than the adults — as they always do. Traditions do not disappear when they change; they change the way language, technology, science and fashion change, one December at a time. The proponents needed — and deserve — patience and respect while adjusting; the opponents deserved the recognition that they were never attacking Sinterklaas at all, only asking not to be caricatured within him.

In the end, it was never really about who is a proponent or an opponent of Zwarte Piet. It is about how we react to one another, how we listen to one another, how we approach one another — the same questions I have carried with me ever since, as a multilingual woman who has always lived between cultures. If we can answer them well, we will not just fix a holiday. We might just depolarise a society — and get back to the part that was never in dispute: celebrating all our traditions, and our children's happiness.
