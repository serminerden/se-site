Group Project: Designing and Piloting a Language Test

Abstract

A group project designing a language test for adult students preparing for the Cambridge Advanced exam - covering test purpose and usefulness, the design of speaking, reading, listening and writing items, the pilot test, and guidelines for users. Written with Un Lai, Shuai Xie and Yi Liu.

The Rationale for the Pilot Test

The Test Takers

The content of the test is based on adult students (aged 20-35 years old) at TAFE Queensland who will be attending a pathway course, which includes cookery, automobile services, and carpentry, after passing at least four tests in a 12-week Cambridge Advanced (CAE) course. The class demographic is broad as the students are from Thailand, Japan, Brazil, Colombia, Chile, Spain, Italy, Croatia, the Philippines, and Indonesia. The learners were in a 12-week First Certificate in English (FCE) course and had to pass at least four exams prior to entering the CAE class. Therefore, the learners are expected to have prior knowledge of the type of testing materials in this course. The content of the test will be designed to assess the students' language proficiency in areas that are relevant to their intended pathway course, such as reading, listening, writing, and speaking skills. The content of the test will also be aligned with the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) levels, which is the recognized standard for language proficiency assessment in Cambridge testing criteria. In determining the test content, a checklist has been implemented, and it includes important considerations such as validity, reliability, authenticity, practicality, and impact (see appendix 1). The test content has been designed to meet these criteria, ensuring that the test accurately measures the students' language proficiency, is fair and unbiased, and provides a useful tool for evaluating their progress.

1.1.2 Textbook and Test Book

The 12-week CAE course at TAFE is 5 days a week for 4 hours a day. The teachers use the Advanced Expert coursebook to teach the regular grammar, listening, speaking, reading, and writing components, which are aligned with the CAE exam (Bell & Gower, 2014).

On Fridays, students practice speaking in the form of presentations, or speaking test practice as illustrated in Figure 1. The weekly tests are administered on Mondays, which are from the Cambridge Advanced in English Test Book (Assessment, 2016). Test Content

1.2.1 Test Purpose

Instead of focusing on the content of each unit, TAFE teachers align the test numbers from the test book with the unit numbers from the coursebook when preparing students for the CAE test. This is because the actual CAE test is designed to be random, and the tests in the test book cover the original components of the exam. As a result, this approach allows for a better alignment with the test format and provides students with a more realistic practice experience. In preparation for the Certificate in Advanced English (CAE) exam, TAFE instructors administer mock tests under authentic exam conditions in weeks 1, 6, and 12 of the course. While the CAE exam itself is randomized, students must pass a minimum of 4 out of the 12 tests in the course to progress to the pathway course of their choice at TAFE. A careful analysis of past test scores revealed that unit 5 had consistently yielded the lowest scores. To address this issue, we took a meticulous approach to redesign the test, focusing on assessing the students' language proficiency in areas that were relevant to the unit they were studying. Our primary objective was to ensure that the test items accurately reflected the level of difficulty, degree of clarity, and appropriateness of the material covered in the unit. Our aim was to provide students with a more effective learning experience that would better prepare them for the pathway course. The differences between the two tests will be explained in detail within the various skill sections. The administration of the test was thoughtfully designed to ensure its reliability and validity. As such, the grammar component of the test was excluded since the reading and listening sections already incorporate multiple-choice, short-answer, and open-ended questions to assess learners’ grammatical skills. This deliberate decision was made to provide an equitable and impartial evaluation of the students' language proficiency based solely on what they have learned in the unit, rather than utilizing generalised tests that are commonly employed at TAFE Queensland. The test's reliability will be ensured through the use of standardized administration conditions and established procedures for scoring and reporting the results, as specified in the Cambridge English Qualifications Handbook for Teachers (Cambridge Assessment English, 2015).

1.2.2 Overview of the Test

The aim of the formative assessment is to assess the English language proficiency and academic knowledge of students who have finished Unit 5. The test is divided into four sections, namely listening, reading, speaking, and writing, and is modelled after the components of CAE. The successful completion of four out of twelve tests is a requirement for students seeking admission to a TAFE course, thereby demonstrating their English proficiency. The primary objective of the pilot test is to assist teachers in monitoring their students' English language development after completion of Unit 5, and to offer valid washback that encourages the enhancement of both students' four language skills and teachers' teaching methodologies. To assess students' language proficiency, a performance-based model has been implemented, as per Fulcher's (2010) recommendations. This model is suitable for evaluating learners' intended communicative effect and their achievement in communicative abilities. To measure the test's usefulness, the CEFR framework was chosen as the basis for assessment and results measurement, as it aligns with the language functions and communicative situations learners can handle and produce confidently. The framework provides students with a clear understanding of their strengths and areas for improvement, facilitating their language development effectively.

1.3 Test Usefulness

The purpose of the language proficiency test is to evaluate the learners' aptitude in English. In light of this, we have chosen to modify the original CAE exam structure to better align with the learners' language skills. To comprehensively evaluate learners' language proficiency, we have included all four areas of listening, speaking, reading, and writing. The original test is geared towards advanced learners who have completed the entire course, and therefore may not be suitable for the current level of the learners. To address this, we have adjusted the level of difficulty of the test questions to match the current proficiency level of the learners. To ensure the validity and relevance of the revised content, learners will initially take the original test, and the modifications will be based on their actual test results. The revised tests are aligned with the themes of the selected unit, which is centred on "controversy," and include engaging content to stimulate learners' interest. In order to achieve the learning objectives of this unit, we have placed particular emphasis on the speaking and writing tests. The speaking test items have been modified to address controversial topics, while also reflecting the themes of the selected unit on commonly discussed issues, such as the environment and climate. Similarly, the writing tests have been revised to feature travel-related contexts, which students are likely to encounter in the classroom. The revised listening and reading tests aim to highlight controversy by updating the questions and texts with themes that reflect current societal concerns around ecological and social development, particularly in Part 6 of the reading test. This section presents a reflective article on the impact of human activity on climate change, using examples of environmental pollution and posing thought-provoking questions. Completing these tests will not only challenge candidates' abilities to comprehend, infer, and analyse information but also encourage them to think critically about the real-world problems raised in the materials and appreciate the diverse cultures represented. In addition, some uplifting stories included in the tests may inspire candidates and evoke a sense of wonder about the world. As previously discussed, learners in the classroom originate from diverse backgrounds and possess varying levels of learning abilities, as well as diverse career aspirations. As a result, these factors necessitate the incorporation of an adaptive testing framework that is mindful of the learners' individual needs. To this end, the speaking tests are tailored to account for learners' proficiency levels, where the examiners use varying assessment criteria to promote a constructive learning experience that helps to alleviate anxiety and encourage optimal performance. Similarly, the writing test is designed to match the learners' current level of knowledge and ability, thus fostering confidence in their academic writing skills. This approach ensures that the assessment is both fair and effective in facilitating learners' progress towards their academic goals. The entire test covers the assessment of the four communicative competencies, and each part of the test examines at least two communicative competencies simultaneously. Our tests mimic the skill mobilization of real-world language use as closely as possible so that learners to gain comprehensive English language development in one test. The time for implementing the test will also be planned by the teacher according to the teaching plan. The test will be divided into four parts, the time for each part is: Speaking (16 minutes), Listening (40 minutes), Reading (1 hour 30 minutes), and Writing (1 hour). Due to the long-time of each section, it is difficult to complete all the tests in one class, The tests will be completed separately in the class time according to the class type, which can ensure that each test can have sufficient time. To ensure test usefulness, the checklist of Bachman and Palmer (1996, pp. 150-155) is used for evaluation (See Appendix 1).

1.4 The Pilot Test

When designing a language proficiency test, it is crucial to consider the five principles of test development, including validity, reliability, practicality, authenticity, washback, and transparency. Validity refers to the test's ability to accurately measure the construct it claims to measure. A valid test should measure the targeted language skill without introducing other confounding factors as we mentioned above. Reliability, on the other hand, refers to the test's consistency in producing similar results when administered multiple times or by different raters. A reliable test should produce consistent results, regardless of the rater or the occasion on which it is administered, which is why we hope that the teachers at TAFE will take into account our pilot test and keep using it in the future to verify these results. The test's practicality is another key consideration, as it should be easy to administer, score, and interpret by those who need to use the test results, which is why we have consulted with the FCE and CAE teachers at TAFE before we distributed our test to the students. They gave us valid feedback to readjust some of our question formulations and corrected some of the question structures. The test's authenticity is also important, as it should simulate real-life tasks and contexts that are meaningful and relevant to the learner. This is the main reason why we have developed our pilot test to accommodate the learners and give them a test which resembled their prior learning. In addition to this, the test's positive washback can motivate learners to learn the material being tested, leading to improved teaching and learning. Finally, the test's transparency is essential, as it should be clear and easily understood by learners and other stakeholders, reducing confusion and increasing the usefulness of the test results. By taking into account these factors, language proficiency tests can be designed to accurately and reliably assess a learner's language skills in a practical, authentic, motivating, and transparent way. As such, the language proficiency assessment for this unit comprises four parts: listening, reading, speaking, and writing, and has been designed in accordance with these six principles of test development. The selection of Unit 5 "Controversy" (See appendix 2) of the coursebook aims to test the students' competence in the four skills and their understanding of the unit's content. By administering this test, students can assess their level of competency in the four skills and the knowledge covered in the unit, identify their areas of strength and weakness, and prepare for future tests and assignments. Adhering to these principles ensures that the test will accurately and reliably measure the students' language proficiency while being practical, authentic, motivating, and transparent in its design.

2. Item design

Overview Speaking Unit 5 of the Advanced Expert Coursebook is about Controversy. The topics mentioned in the unit are predominantly about the environment, however, test 5 focuses on isolated places, painting, and what sort of help people need in life. The topics in the test measure illocutionary competence, whereas a speaking construct should refer to the ability, knowledge, and processes involved in the production of spoken language (Fulcher, 2003). These skills include pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary, fluency, and discourse management. A speaking construct is typically assessed through tasks or activities that require the learners to use these abilities in order to communicate effectively in a given situation. Fulcher (2003) emphasizes that it is important to define the construct clearly to develop valid and reliable assessments of speaking ability. This is why we have adapted the test according to the unit taught in the prior week. The redesigned test involves controversial topics about the environment, climate change, and problems people discuss in general. The students have also watched two documentaries related to controversial topics (i.e., The World’s Water Crisis, and The Game Changers), these documentaries discuss the importance of water to humans and the planet, and how the planet is running out of drinkable water, which is linked to the meat industry. As homework, they watched the second documentary which is about athletes who have opted for a vegan diet and how their bodies can perform without meat. This documentary is controversial and is linked to the meat industry as the cause of deforestation, the reduction of oxygen, and the increase of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.

2.3 Listening

2.3.1 Original Listening Test

Brown and Abeywickrama (2010) mention that the process of listening performance itself is an invisible and inaudible process from the auditory signal to the inner meaning of the ear and brain. It is a learning performance that cannot be directly observed. In addition, more than 45% of human communication is spent listening. This shows the importance of listening skills in overall language ability. Therefore, in order to test students' listening ability at this stage, teachers must observe students' listening ability through listening tests. To test the listening ability of learners in this stage. Unit 5 of the textbook focuses on the decline of the bee population. Using an audio clip, students are asked to answer short questions based on the listening material. Next, fill in the blanks according to the recording. These two basic exercises mainly examine students' language competence and strategic competence. One is to test students' ability to summarise listening materials; the other is to test students' ability to process vocabulary spelling and key information in listening materials. For the listening practice in the textbook, this listening test mainly sets questions on language competence and strategic competence. To pass the listening test, students should first master the vocabulary spelling of the learning stage. Second, students should be able to understand long conversations or speeches. And be able to understand the key message of the speaker. In the first part of the test, students were given three conversations and asked to choose the answers to the questions based on the conversations. The conversation included a conversation between a student teacher and her tutor about teaching, a discussion between a couple of friends about a concert, and their thoughts on a new company policy. In the original test, students need to quickly understand and discriminate the meaning of the question stem and options. Due to the short examination time and weak text comprehension ability of students, the correct rate of equalisation is about 50%, and some questions get high scores because the question stem is simple and easy to understand. The second part of the test provided a speech to the scientist addressing the robot he had created. Fill in the blanks according to the recording. First, test students' vocabulary and spelling abilities. Second, it examines the ability of students to capture key information. Thirdly, the question is rewritten according to the audio, and the student's ability to replace synonyms in English is tested. In the original test, students performed well on some of the simpler words but made more spelling mistakes on the more complex words. At the same time, some key information captured for context understanding, students' performance ability is poor. Unable to achieve quick semantic conversion and positioning. The third part of the test provides a recording of an interview discussing the history of the London Underground. This part examines students' ability to paraphrase sentences and paraphrase conversations. Listen to the recording to determine the correct choice. This part of the question is easy to understand, so the score is relatively good. Individuals with bewildering topics stem, the overall level of students is poor. The fourth part of the test provides five short voice excerpts of people talking about museums they have visited. This part requires students to perform out-of-order matching, which contains multiple interfering items that are not given. This part tests students' ability to capture key information and answer strategies. Because of the way the question is set, the visual effect is confused, and the question item is all paraphrased. The students' lack of problem-solving strategies and language ability resulted in very low scores in this part of the test. It can be seen that students' spelling ability is relatively good, but the complex vocabulary is still unfamiliar. Some words are simply misspelled but heard correctly, which suggests that students should also improve their vocabulary proficiency. In addition, students' ability to summarise the listening text and capture key information is insufficient. The revised test will reduce the difficulty of distracting items and interpretations in the questions.

2.4 Writing

2.4.1 Original Writing Test

Writing is considered a basic skill to test the ability of second language learners. At present, learners are still in the stage of knowledge accumulation. In order to successfully pass the four tests in 12 weeks, learners need to practice writing during the course to improve their specific English ability. The purpose of the writing test is to help learners acquire the ability to write academic essays. The test is conducted in the context of the students having acquired the relevant knowledge, the learners have also received writing instruction before this task, and the format of the test follows the requirements of the course. In addition, the topic introduction also provides students with relevant prompts about the topic to help them successfully understand the question. The question of the task is very clear, and students can develop their own ideas within the prescribed limits. This open writing test not only ensures that learners can use their knowledge to achieve their goals as much as possible but also learn the interactivity of the test. The original writing test is mainly to test the learners' strategic competence and grammatical competence. The item requires students to clarify their statements on a given task and provide reasons to support their opinions. This is the primary training method for students' writing ability in this course. Students can use their language knowledge in an appropriate way to achieve their communication goals, which is a manifestation of strategic competence. Bachman and Palmer (1996) state that strategic competence not only provides practice for language knowledge and the external environment but also provides an effective connection between language knowledge and subject knowledge. As for grammatical competence, learners need to effectively combine the language system and semantic system in the process of completing the essay, which can also be said to be the combination of words and grammar. Writing can assess how learners use the grammatical rules of the target language with different structures and can also assess the accuracy of learners' vocabulary usage in a specific topic. In terms of authenticity, although this test does not reflect the real world, as a writing test, we can consider it in terms of how it helps learners in social interaction. If a test is claimed to be authentic, it is likely that the test was developed in the real world (Brown & Abeywickrama, 2010) Learners are likely to need to express their views on this topic. Spack (1988) referred to the process of learning to write in an academic setting as one of "guiding ESL students into a community of academic discourse". Writing, as the written version of speaking, is almost the same. Therefore, the learner's ideas in writing can also be utilized in practical communication. The writing test is completed in one teaching period with the other three tests, which is very convenient for both students and teachers. Also, we only selected one item of the writing test, so the test time is set at 1 hour, which is quite sufficient for students. On the basis of the original scale, the scoring criteria also refers to CEFR to provide learners with accurate feedback. Therefore, testing is very useful. The results of the original test did not receive good expectations. Among the 15 articles, only 7 students answered the questions clearly, and 5 students did not complete the test. There were also some mistakes in the use of grammar and syntax, and the average score of all students was only 3 points (out of 5). According to this, we provided feedback for each test paper, clearly listing the problems that students have. We also collected feedback from students about the test: they said that they did not understand the requirements of the questions correctly, so they did not answer the questions. In addition, the students who have not finished the essay are new members of this course, and they have not learned a lot of knowledge. Therefore, the test was revised in response to student feedback and confirmation of unit content.

3.2.3 Metalinguistic Awareness of Learners

In order for the learners to understand their feedback and learning outcomes, students are asked to record the speaking test and transcribe their part of the speaking. Students then have to highlight their own errors and correct them with what they should have said (see an example in figure 7). The implementation of self-assessment as a method for learners to evaluate their own language proficiency and to develop their metacognitive abilities to self-appraise and self-regulate. The most effective and meaningful self-assessment methods involve learners setting their own goals, monitoring their progress, and reflecting on their own learning experiences (Brindley, 1989). These methods have been integrated into language learning and teaching and are accompanied by feedback and guidance from the teacher. By providing learners with suggestions for improvement and helping them develop a more accurate understanding of their language proficiency, the teacher can enhance the effectiveness of self-assessment. Ultimately, self-assessment should be an ongoing process that enables learners to take ownership of their language learning and development. By engaging in self-assessment, learners can become more aware of their strengths and weaknesses, set achievable goals, and reflect on their progress (Brindley, 1989). This holistic approach allows learners to listen to themselves and enables learners to experience cognitive and socio-effective processes of listening comprehension, while writing down what they are listening to, which is a form of a dictation task, reinforcing them to write, to then read and correct their own errors while working on a speaking-related task (Goh & Burns, 2012). 4. Test Specifications

4.1 Test General Specifications

The purpose of this test is to assess learners' learning outcomes and communicative competence after studying this unit. Bachman (1990) believed that: testing is a kind of measurement and a way to evaluate teaching behaviour. Students need to pass the test of this course to prove that they have the English knowledge and abilities required for future study, life, and work. Periodic tests help students understand their learning effects and make progress through teacher feedback. The test is designed according to the current level of the students. Learners can put the learned knowledge into practice through the test of the four language skills and consolidate it. Speaking and writing will achieve effective output in real-world communication, students can express their opinions fluently and accurately and analyse them reasonably. With the help of listening and reading, learners can contact learning materials other than textbooks to expand their knowledge system. In order to make the best use of what students have learned, the test has been revised according to the theme of this unit. It is expected that learners can understand "controversy" and accurately and effectively produce the key knowledge of this unit in the process of communication. This test will be done in several classes, according to the teaching plan. In the classroom environment, students will experience less tension and maximize the learning effect.

4.2.1 Test Purpose Speaking

The students in the original test and pilot test were from two different classes and teachers. The original test was conducted between the 21st and 25th of April 2023, whereas the pilot test was redesigned and administered between the 1st of May till the 5th of May 2023.

As mentioned in the rationale, the learners are studying at TAFE to get into one of the pathway courses it offers. In order to do so, learners are expected to pass four out of twelve tests. The speaking component is one of the important tasks as learners are expected to communicate with Australian students during their pathway courses, work on group assignments, and present various topics in their field. TAFE expects the learners to have a minimum overall score of 15 out of 25, which is 60%.

4.2.2 Test Description Speaking

As illustrated in part 3.2 of this paper, the speaking test consists of three parts, each assessing different communicative competencies. In Part 1, the interlocutor engages in a brief exchange with each candidate to evaluate their use of general interactional and social language. However, this part was omitted in the pilot test. Part 2 involves an individual 'long turn' for each candidate, where they describe two out of three presented photographs, testing their ability to organize discourse, compare and describe visual information, and express opinions and speculations (Cambridge Assessment English, 2015). Part 3 requires the candidates to engage in a two-way conversation, based on spoken instructions and written stimuli, to complete a discussion and decision-making task. The conversation is divided into two phases and aims to assess the candidates' ability to sustain interaction, exchange ideas, express and justify opinions, agree or disagree, suggest, speculate, evaluate, and negotiate (Cambridge Assessment English, 2015). Lastly, Part 4 is a discussion on topics related to the collaborative task, with a focus on expressing and justifying opinions, agreeing or disagreeing, and speculating. The aim of these four parts is to assess multiple communicative competencies and simulate the mobilization of language skills in real-world contexts, contributing to the development of comprehensive English language proficiency among learners (Cambridge Assessment English, 2015). The timing of each part is predetermined, and the test is administered in separate sessions to ensure sufficient time for each section.

4.2.3 Scoring Pronunciation

Fulcher (2003) suggests that pronunciation can be tested through the use of minimal pairs, where learners are asked to distinguish between two similar sounds that are contrastive in the target language (e.g., Chip/Cheap). Other approaches could include assessing learners' ability to produce certain sounds, stress patterns, or connected speech (i.e., Don't trust him he lies/ opticians can heal eyes). Testing involves a range of tasks, such as paraphrasing sentences or words in the questions asked, describing pictures or scenes, or showing the ability to maintain interactive conversations. These test items are tested in parts 2, 3, and 4 of the Speaking Test, and we have kept the original scoring rubric or the CAE test as it is reliable. Additionally, it was recommended by the teachers at TAFE to not change it, so they could use the test in the future.

4.2.4 Scoring Interactive Communication / Fluency

Testing learners' ability to produce language smoothly and at a natural pace, without hesitations or pauses, this item can be tested through the use of timed speaking tasks, where learners are given a set amount of time to speak on a given topic (Fulcher, 2003). Add to this, parts 3 and 4 of the Speaking test are to reinforce students to have conversations where they are encouraged to interact with the interlocutor and another student to maintain a flow of conversation. The main focus of the interlocutor will be to look out for the learners’ appropriate use of interaction linkers, initiate and respond appropriately, and negotiate toward an outcome.

4.2.5 Scoring Accuracy

According to Fulcher (2003), accuracy can be tested by assessing learners' grammatical and lexical precision, as well as their ability to use appropriate discourse markers in context. Testing could involve tasks that require learners to produce grammatically and semantically correct sentences, which could involve eliciting language from learners in response to specific prompts or situations, such as describing a picture, telling a story, or interaction sequences, as demonstrated in parts 2, 3, and 4. The effective use of these strategies is important for successful communication, as these strategies help learners to overcome difficulties in expressing themselves and make use of the language resources they have available. They can also help learners to negotiate meaning, repair communication breakdowns, and manage their speech production in real-time. Effective use of communication strategies can therefore lead to more successful and confident communication, which can in turn lead to increased motivation and language accuracy in the L2 (Fulcher, 2003). Incorporating the notion of the structure of conversation into the assessment of speaking involves assessing how well the speaker is able to maintain a coherent and organized conversation. This includes the ability to initiate and respond to conversation appropriately, the ability to maintain a topic, and the ability to use appropriate discourse markers and other features of spoken language to signal the structure of the conversation. To test the ability to use discourse markers effectively, the assessment could focus on the appropriate use of these markers. Discourse markers are words or phrases that indicate the relationship between sentences or clauses in a conversation (e.g., however, whereas, so, or for example). Skilled use of discourse markers can enhance the coherence and organization of a conversation, as well as support the listener in understanding the intended meaning of the speaker. Moreover, social and pragmatic knowledge is also needed to show competence by initiating and responding in conversation and how to use appropriate register and tone. Testing interaction competence involves a range of features (e.g., turn-taking strategies, intonation stress, sustaining a conversation, and using idiomatic language and figurative language).
