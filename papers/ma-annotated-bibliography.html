Annotated Bibliography: The Limits of Frequency in Second Language Acquisition

Abstract

An annotated bibliography of three key studies on whether frequent exposure to a second language can ever fully deliver native-like competence - and where ingrained first-language knowledge continues to shape production.

Article 1

Von Stutterheim, C., Lambert, M., & Gerwien, J. (2021). Limitations on the role of frequency in L2 acquisition. Language and Cognition, online ahead of print.doi:10.1017/langcog.2021.5 The authors acknowledge one of the commonly accepted theories of L2 acquisition is one based on the frequency of exposure to the L2. This study offers a counterpoint to this view and explores the limitations of frequency for SLA. The authors are open about their contrarian views on this subject. Their research shows that language competence cannot be attained through frequent exposure alone. They suggest, is due to ingrained implicit language knowledge of the L1. In other words, frequency of exposure is not the main contributor to acquiring full competence in L2 acquisition. The paper highlights that other studies judge L2 acquisition based on storage and production (in a controlled and scripted context) of the L2. Learners are entirely capable of learning and producing lexical items, idiomatic expressions and have knowledge of collocations when being tested in the classroom. Authors point out that unscripted, creative language use is an under-researched area. Additionally, producing these during natural and spontaneous dialogue does not occur when linking linguistic categories and specific language sets. Two groups were compared in this empirical study. A monolingual group of English, French, and German. The second group’s L1 was French and L2 of either English or German (C1 level), they responded in their respective L2. The study is based on these languages due to their different patterns in spatial relevance in motion and the use of path verbs to describe directed motion.

Participants were recorded while being shown six-second motion videos of a scenario and asked to produce unscripted language describing the situation. The monolinguals used their native L1 linguistic constructions: path verbs and directional adjuncts for French; dynamic event frames with manner verbs and directional complements for German and English. The bilingual group reverted to their L1 linguistic constructions while describing events using the L2. These results support the hypothesis and provide evidence that L1 has an influence when producing L2 regardless of the frequency of exposure to the L2. However, this study cannot be applied to all languages, solely to French L1 speakers.

Article 2

Crosthwaite, P. (2014). Definite discourse-new reference in L1 and L2: A study of bridging in Mandarin, Korean, and English. Language Learning, 64, 456–492. This study conducted two experiments to investigate whether L2 English speakers with native Mandarin (Chinese) and Korean backgrounds have potential L1 transfer effects and if that effect has an impact on the learnability of the L2. Mandarin and Korean were selected for this research as both languages do not have an article system. However, Mandarin does use a quantifying system (i.e., numeral + classifier). In both experimental groups, the study used 20 participants per language group, and all were presented with the same picture sequence and asked to produce a story based on what they saw.

The first experiment examined data from native speakers (i.e., English, Mandarin, and Korean). The native English speakers told a story using indefinite articles when introducing nouns. However, would use definite articles when a specific noun was present in the pictures (e.g., a boy=> the boy). The Mandarin native speakers would use numerals in similar situations (e.g., one boy). The Koreans would simply use bare nominals (e.g., boy). The second experiment analysed data collected from English L2 learners, whose native languages were Mandarin and Korean. These learners were at various Common European Framework of References (CEFR) levels of English. This was done to show at which level the learners would start using articles in discourse. The main purpose was to see if the L1 would either help, hinder or have no effect during the learning process when learning articles in the L2. Due to numeral identifiers in Mandarin, participants were able to understand and use articles at a lower level (A2). Korean learners were unable to use articles until B2 level, due to bare nominals in the L1. The results illustrate that using articles in the L2 is dependent on the L1 descriptors. This sheds light on the impact of positive and negative transfer effects of the L1 on the L2 with Mandarin and Korean participants.

Article 3

Boroditsky, L. (2001). Does language shape thought?: Mandarin and English speakers' conceptions of time. Cognitive psychology, 43(1), 1-22.

The main aim of this research is to understand if the languages and metaphors people use, shape the way they think. Boroditsky (2000, 2001) questions whether this metaphor use causes differences in how people think differently about time, short and long term, in different languages, and if people can rewire the way they think. For this study, the author used English and Mandarin speakers as they both use horizontal metaphors (i.e., before/after) but Mandarin also uses vertical metaphors (i.e., up/down). All participants were university students. The first experiment of this study compared native English speakers to Mandarin speakers with a mean of 12.8 years of English acquisition. All participants were first primed with spatial questions before being asked the “real” or “target” question. The response times of only the “target” questions were recorded. As expected, English speakers responded faster when presented with horizontal primes and Mandarin speakers responded faster when presented with vertical primes. In the second experiment, the participants were native Mandarin speakers who learned English later in life. The author investigated “vertical bias”, that is the propensity to “think vertically”. Materials and design were the same as in the first experiment. Results illustrated that the semantic bias about thinking of time vertically had increased in participants who learned English later in life. In the third experiment of this study, native English speakers were shortly primed to learn a new way to talk about time. They were presented with the new system by giving examples (e.g., Monday is above / higher than Tuesday). Materials and design were the same as the previous two experiments. After brief training in their own language, the participants were

able to answer the vertically timed questions as quickly as their native horizontal timings. Thus, illustrating that learning a new way to speak in the L1 can restructure thinking. These results suggest that language can guide the way we see the world around us and our L1 can control/limit the way we express ourselves in the L2.

Comparison of three articles

These articles show a degree of the L1’s influence on the production of the L2 at a cognitive level. The investigative methods and approaches differ between the articles. I will compare and contrast the articles in terms of procedures, methodology, and findings. Firstly, articles 1 and 3 conduct their empirical studies based on advanced level L2 learners, whereas article 2 measures all CEFR levels. Articles 1 and 2 employ storytelling in their procedures as all languages have a distinct way to tell stories due to their language-specific syntax (Slobin, 1996). The studies create a controlled environment where participants must produce a story without prompts. This reinforces natural language production where storytelling in the L2 leads to reversion to L1 syntax (Slobin, 1996). Article 2 uses storytelling via pictures whereas article 1 opts for storytelling via video fragments. Article 3 employed a non-language-specific task to focus on subconscious decision-making. Secondly, Articles 1 and 2 opted for qualitative data through observation by recording and transcribing the storytelling. All articles selected quantitative data as the experimentation was controlled and manipulated in a university setting and applied corpora to compare monolinguals against English L2 learners.

All articles used small sample sizes of university students to conduct their research. This makes it easier to find participants and create homogenous groups to avoid variables. However, this approach restricts the participants (ergo data) to a single group, excluding large sections of the populace. Finally, all three articles provide evidence that although people can acquire additional languages, their L1 remains subconsciously dominant. Even though all articles conducted research based on the influence of the L1 on the L2, all three investigated different areas of influence. Article 1 illustrates that the role of frequency of exposure to the L2 is irrelevant as the L1 drives the L2 in production when participants must produce language naturally. Article 2 provides evidence of positive and negative transfer of the L1 in L2 learning and the learners’ process and progress. Article 3 argues that the L1 has control over the way humans think and see the world. In other words, people can change the language they use, but cannot change the way they think. In summary, these studies are relevant and applicable in the field of Psycholinguistics, ESL, and SLA. Although the research would have benefitted from larger sample sizes, the different approaches are sophisticated and provide explicit knowledge in terms of how we see the world around us through our L1. The papers present avenues for expansion into comparisons between other languages and language families. (Wordcount 1417)
