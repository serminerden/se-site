How Do (Adult) Second Language Learners Begin Acquiring a Second Language, How Do They Progress, and What Challenges Do They Face?

Abstract

Drawing on second language acquisition theory and personal language learning experience, this essay traces the adult learner’s journey - from the initial state through interlanguage development - and the role of feedback, input and processing in making progress.

There are various theories on how learners acquire their second language (L2) based on their age, first language (L1), aptitude, motivation, learning strategies, etc. However, the issues that learners need to overcome and how they make progress in their language learning can be looked at on a macroscale and at an individual level. This essay will explain how L2 learners start their language process, how their language evolves over time, and the issues the learners have to overcome in order to acquire a second language.

Individuals attempting to learn a second language begin their journey in what can be described as their initial state. The “initial state” refers to the starting point of the learner’s second language (L2) journey and is unique per learner. Several factors constitute the initial state. The first language (L1) of the learner sets the context for their L2 learning experience. They know what language is, how language functions, and can communicate in their L1. Universal Grammar (UG) defines variation between languages by using parameters. Different languages have different parameters (Gass & Selinker, 2001), which provide information about the relatedness or differences between the learners’ L1 and L2. If L1 and L2 are similar (e.g., both L1 and L2 are Germanic languages) then the learner can access L1 parameters during their L2 learning, which would result in faster language processing and progression in their language learning. Conversely, if L1 and L2 are sufficiently different, alternative mechanisms are used (VanPatten, 2015) and progression may be slower. For example, in high school, I learned German very quickly and became a fluent speaker because it is related to my L1 (Dutch) and L3 (English). However, I had to drop French because there was no relation to my prior language knowledge, and I struggled to progress in my learning. The reason these parameters become fixed over time has been linked to the critical period (Gass & Selinker, 2001). The critical period is the brain lateralization process children go through when they reach puberty (Lenneberg, 1967). Lenneberg (1967) suggests that language learning becomes more difficult and effortful as the brain starts focussing on other biological factors, which become more important in the brain. Even though the window of opportunity for language learning closes with the critical period, it can be said that L2 learners still have partial access to UG through their L1 (Gass & Selinker, 2001), as all learners know the forms and functions of their L1 within their own language parameters. Second Language Acquisition (SLA) depends on learning the forms of the L2 to carry out the functions of their L1. L2 development is driven by universal principles (Klein & Perdue, 1992:313), which are independent of the influence of the native speaker or the L2. Each language has a different set of forms, however, functions are universal (Halliday, 1973, 1975). If SLA relied only on forms of the L1, learners would all make different mistakes, as determined by these L1 forms. However, what occurs with SLA is an unknown factor, taking place in the minds of learners that all go through the same developmental stages, this is similar to First Language Acquisition (Klein & Perdue, 1992). To express the same meaning learned in the L1, the learner is forced to learn the set of forms of the L2 to carry out the pragmatic functions of the L2 (Saville-Troike, 2012). This does not mean the learners can reach communicative competence, because sometimes we need the language form to gain pragmatic meaning (Saville-Troike, 2012). The advantage of functional account is that functional learning enables adult L2 learners to get meaning across without focussing on the form of the language. This promotes fluency, and the learner feels they are progressing in their learning because they can interact and communicate without hesitation by reaching communicative competence (Hymes, 1974).

There are different phases to language learning as L2 learners progress in their L2 journey. Selinker’s (1972) Interlanguage (IL) theory argues L2 learners use the language they know because they are not familiar with all the forms and meanings of the L2. IL entails a combination of the learner’s L1 patterns, expanding with patterns of the L2 by expressing what they know from both languages using the L2. There are systematic rules in IL and learners use those rules until they notice their errors within their language use, which helps them correct these errors and they progress in their language learning. This suggests that learners need to notice and understand the gap in their language abilities in order to have comprehensible output (Schmidt, 1990). Interaction is the foundation of human communication which fuels language learning. Long (1981, 1985) argues interaction helps the learners to receive interactionally modified input as it makes learners notice the gaps in their language, helping them correct errors which allows learning progression. More interaction taking place means more opportunities for learners to negotiate for meaning (Long, 1996). Negotiation for meaning could also be formulated through error correction in the form of recasts, explicit error correction, and prompts (Lyster & Saito, 2010). For example, a learner who never uses articles (e.g., “what is problem?); if the learner is not made aware of the error, the learner may not notice the gap in their language (Erden, Ripple). Interacting with the learner by reformulating the error causes the learner to understand the gap which turns it into interactionally modified input, and the learner adapts the correct form. The way these errors are corrected is crucial. Mitchell & Myles (2004) suggest error correction could lead to a negative learning experience, and the way error correction is delivered is important. The importance of purposeful interaction and negative feedback seems to change in the learner’s different developmental stages (Mitchell & Myles, 2004). For instance, I started learning Spanish when I was 30, realising at the time that I could not progress past the present tense. Every time I was corrected, embarrassment and fear dissuaded me from speaking up in class. However, when I first started my undergraduate degree in English Language and Linguistics, I tended to make mistakes in my writing with words which are phonetically related (e.g., flu / flew). In those situations, I actually appreciated being notified of my errors. When learners reach proficiency in their language knowledge, once notified of an error, they can replace errors faster with the correct version as I did and still do. The feedback learners receive allows them to notice and think about their errors, reformulate the sentence used, and reflect on the feedback regardless of it being positive or negative (Swain & Lapkin, 1995). Moreover, it pushes learners and provides opportunities to experiment with new forms and structures in the L2. This process is fundamental in their language progress as it offers occasions to reflect on, discuss and analyse their language problems implicitly and explicitly.

SLA is influenced significantly by the processing of language and how it is obtained, which occurs implicitly and explicitly. Schmidt (1990) illustrates that explicit learning happens through conscious processing, which means learners are aware of the learning process. However, implicit learning happens subconsciously when information is processed without the learners’ knowledge. For example, a native speaker knows how to categorise adjectives implicitly (e.g., an unusual small thin round old red French plastic general-purpose, cleaning brush), but would not be able to explain how or why they order their sentence that way as it is implicit knowledge. Whereas language teachers would be able to explain the order as they have learned the metalanguage explicitly (i.e., determiner, opinion, size, physical quality, shape, age, colour, origin, material, type, purpose, noun). This is when implicit knowledge is transferred to explicit knowledge. However, for an L2 learner who becomes an ESL teacher, it works the other way around. They learn the order explicitly through practice and repetition which becomes implicit knowledge over time, thus demonstrating that L2 learners can turn explicit knowledge into implicit knowledge through receiving comprehensible input which could lead to comprehensible intake through focussed practice (VanPatten, 2015). The level of input frequency is crucial in SLA and cannot be learned by abstract rules (Ellis, 2005). The way the input is controlled can maximise the input opportunity for both explicit and implicit learning to occur. In order to make sure that learners turn explicit knowledge in implicit knowledge, the information needs to be stored in the long-term memory. This can only happen if information processed accordingly. Pienemann (1998) argues that production and comprehension of the L2 can only take place if the learner has developed a number of processing procedures. This will help the learner to store the attained knowledge in long-term-memory.

In summation, although L2 learners begin their journey with L1 knowledge, this is no guarantee they become proficient in their L2. As learners undergo developmental phases of their individual language learning, issues can be overcome by noticing their own errors, aided by receiving: Constructive feedback Comprehensible input The right procedures to maximise intake and process language Learner practice and purposeful interactions both inside and outside the classroom help learners progress. Finally, motivation is a large factor in learning an L2 environment; the learner must be dedicated (with teacher / mentor support) in order to achieve the best L2 learning outcomes.
