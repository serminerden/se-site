Research Proposal: Emotion, the First Language and Native-Like Production in a Second Language

Abstract

Even highly proficient second language speakers keep a first-language fingerprint in how they describe the world. This proposal designs a study of Dutch heritage speakers in Australia producing emotion adjectives, asking whether frequency of exposure alone can deliver native-like production.

Introduction

The process of Second Language Acquisition (SLA) and its production has been continually researched for decades. Even when learners master the second language (L2) at an advanced or proficient level, there still seems to be a first language (L1) component which remains present in their minds. As most of our actions are driven by our human emotions, do proficient L2 speakers, influenced by such emotions, fail to follow the specific set of forms and functions which are carried out by native speakers?

Due to globalisation and global warming, migration will only continue to rise in certain parts of the world (IOM, 2021). The aim of this research project is to understand to what extent the L1 influences the emotional thought processes and production of the L2. When communities with different languages and cultural backgrounds have to live and function together as a society, it is important to understand if the frequency of exposure can ultimately override the functions of the L1 in L2 production.

This research will examine the frequency of exposure to the target language (TL) when producing adjectives to describe emotional events. Humans share a common set of emotions (i.e., joy, fear, anger, disgust, sadness, surprise) regardless of the language they speak. However, linguistically, some languages have more or fewer adjectives to describe these emotions, which can result in negative L1 transfer when producing language in the L2 (Boroditsky, 2000, 2001, 2003, 2010). This will be further discussed in the Literature Review.

2. Literature Review

2.1 Do we use the L2 differently based on our L1 and does that change the way we see the world? Language learners, at the beginning of their L2 journey, tend to translate words and expressions literally from their L1. This process changes when learners reach an advanced or proficient level of the L2, as they start thinking in the L2. However, the question arises as to whether it is possible to separate emotional meaning attached to words in the L1 which pre-exist in the lexicon.

Boroditsky, Schmidt, and Phillips (2003) discuss unpublished data indicating that grammatical gender in the L1 alters the representation of objects in the L2. This suggests that language with grammatical gender, assigns gender to objects in the L2 influenced by the participants’ L1. In this empirical study with reference to Konishi (1993), Spanish and German speakers were asked to list three adjectives in English to describe nouns. The objects selected had opposite gender in both languages, and the entire experiment was conducted in English. The participants would choose adjectives in English based on the gender of the noun represented in their L1. For example, when asked to describe a bridge, the German speakers would use feminine adjectives (e.g., beautiful, elegant, and pretty). On the contrary, the Spanish speakers would use masculine words (e.g., dangerous, long, and strong). These results suggest that people are influenced by their linguistic abilities which are linked to mental representations, even when the objects are genderless. This implies when second language learners use English to communicate, the adjectives selected to describe the same object are based on their L1.

When people migrate and have to adapt to a new environment, it is vital to understand the differences between these variables in order to avoid conflict between different social communities. This may be resolved when people are exposed to the new language for a long period of time, which leads to the next question.

2.2 Is frequent exposure to the L2 enough to maintain and produce the L2 like native speakers? SLA is complex as we deal with general individual differences on top of language competence. When learners have learned the forms and functions of the L2 and have full communicative competence, are they capable of producing the L2 as native speakers would? For decades, scholars were in agreement that the frequency of exposure to the TL would be critical to attaining full language competence (Ellis, 2002; Gass & Mackey, 2002). Von Stutterheim et al. (2021) offer a contrastive view by exploring the limits of frequency of exposure as language cannot be attained solely through exposure. The empirical study reveals that learners are fully capable of learning and producing lexical items, idiomatic expressions, and collocations in a scripted and controlled environment (e.g., classroom settings). This allows learners to pass exams and achieve advanced (C1) or proficiency (C2) levels in their L2. However, this does not prove full language competence outside the classroom setting where the learner has to produce language naturally. Unscripted, creative language use, and producing these naturally and spontaneously in dialogue is an under-researched area (von Stutterheim et al., 2021). The participants in this study were shown six-second videos and asked to describe the scene while being recorded. The study compared monolinguals of English, French, and German speakers to French L1 speakers with either advanced level (C1) English or German as their L2. As the authors expected, the monolingual groups would use the native L1 linguistic constructions (i.e., the French opted for path verbs and directional adjuncts; the Germans and English opted for manner verbs and directional complements). The bilinguals reverted to their ingrained implicit knowledge of their L1 when describing events using the L2, concluding that language competence cannot be attained through frequent exposure.

Both studies provide evidence that the L1 interferes when using the L2 in different ways regardless of someone’s level or frequency of exposure. These results open doors for further research and raise new questions.

3. Research Questions

It is evident, based on the research in the literature review, that the L1 has a strong influence on the L2. Languages with opposite grammatical gender cause L2 speakers to use adjectives in correspondence to their L1. The limitations in Boroditsky’s study include a lack of clarity on the level of English that participants had, and the extent of time in which they were exposed to the L2. The participants in von Stutterheim’s study did not have the same frequency of exposure to the TL as each other, nor the same language experience, as some studied abroad for a year, while others worked abroad for two years. There are additional variables between the participants which matters as the sample size is relatively small.

The Dutch and Scandinavians are known for being proficient English speakers. There are unresearched theories about the reasons why. Unresearched theories exist that attempt to explain this phenomenon, due to their school systems, exposure to English television as they use subtitles, or the fact that the languages are related which makes it easier to learn the language due to its parameters (Gass & Selinker, 2001; VanPatten, 2015). On the contrary, this evidence is further proven by the fact that learners of L2s that have vastly different L1s, generally take longer to acquire a second language (VanPatten, 2015; Crosthwaite, 2014).

Would the frequency of exposure to the L2 change the adjectives used by people who have been exposed to the TL for the same length of time? This leads to the following research questions:

To what extent do proficient speakers of an L2 use the most frequent forms used by native speakers when producing adjectives to describe emotional events?

Does the frequency of exposure to the TL have an impact on the way L2 speakers use forms which are frequent in the TL, or do they use the forms in correspondence with their L1 in the TL?

4. Proposed Method

4.1 Context of the Research

Research shows that migration is increasing (IOM, 2021). It will only become more important to create an integration policy which works toward a well-functioning society where people work together in harmony. Countries and cultures who have been through this before provide an ideal environment for this study. In the 1960s a large group of Turkish migrants moved to the Netherlands under the name “guest workers” (Staring, 2014). The name suggests they were there as guests but most of them stayed and raised their families in the Netherlands. Even though the Netherlands has fourth-generation Turkish-Dutch citizens, the relationship between the two communities remains strained (Staring, 2014).

4.2 Selection of Participants

To teach English in the Netherlands, one must pass the Cambridge Proficiency (C2) exam to start a degree in TESOL. The participants will be final year students from the Hogeschool van Amsterdam who have had the same length of exposure to the TL, who have passed the same exam, and are all studying to become TESOL teachers. This is intended to reduce variables and allows for a better comparison of data results.

Group A: 20 Dutch Native Speakers

Group B: 20 Dutch Native Speakers with Turkish Heritage

Group C: 20 English Native Speakers (Control Group)

Data Collection Method

The set up for this experiment will be similar to that of von Stutterheim et al. (2021) with influences of Boroditsky et al (2003). The reason for selecting covert qualitative data is to avoid a scripted environment where participants would select answers as they would do in class, which would give undesirable results. Placing the participants in an unscripted environment will encourage them to produce language naturally and spontaneously (von Stutterheim et al., 2021). The participants will be recorded, and their results will be transcribed.

5. Materials

5.1 Priming of the Participants

The participants will be shown images of people expressing human emotions (i.e., fear, joy, sadness, disgust, anger, surprise). This is also done to eliminate participants who are not able to read human emotions.

5.2 Tasks

The participants will be recorded while being shown ten-second videos of contrasting life experiences (e.g., weddings, graduations, funerals, natural disasters). The situations will be repeated in different scenarios to ensure that the participants are using similar responses in the same situations, this is to verify their results. The participants will be asked to answer the following questions:

What is happening in the video? The reason for this question is to ensure the participants understand the situation.

How do you think the people feel? This is to ensure that the participants focus on the feelings of the people in the video to avoid personal bias.

How does it make you feel? To ensure there is no personal bias towards the previous question. Also, to see if they use different adjectives comparing both questions.

5.3 Data Analysis

The data will be transcribed, and the adjectives will be extracted, processed, and categorised in each group of emotions. The results will then be compared to answer the research questions posed.

To answer research questions 1 and 2, Groups A and B will be compared to the control group. According to streamline theories there should not be any difference as the participants are proficient speakers of the L2. According to von Stutterheim and Boroditsky’s theories, it is expected that Groups A and B will use different adjectives when compared to the Control Group as the L1 will have an influence on their natural L2 production.

To answer research question 2 in-depth with the inbuilt control group of the Turkish-Dutch native speakers, Groups A and B will be compared to one another. In theory, these groups should have similar results as they have learned the L2 under the same conditions (i.e., school system, teaching styles, exposure). However, it is expected based on the literature review that Group A and B will use different adjectives due to the influence of Group B’s heritage language.

6. Conclusion

This study explores the influence of frequency of exposure in SLA. However, there are some limitations to this study. Conducting qualitative research limits researchers with sample size and generalisation of the data cannot occur. In addition, the participants are all university students which does not provide a full or varied representation of outer society. Emotions are personal and due to individual differences; the results may vary. On the contrary, this paper contributes to Learner Corpora, the field of Sociolinguistics, Psycholinguistics, and ESL. It gives insight as to whether immigrant communities who live in one country interact with natives successfully. The insights gained from this study could be used to inform public policy decisions on immigration.
