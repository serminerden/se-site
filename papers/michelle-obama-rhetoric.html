Michelle Obama’s Rhetoric at the 2016 Democratic National Convention

Abstract

A rhetorical and discourse analysis of Michelle Obama’s address at the 2016 Democratic National Convention, described by the Washington Post as the best speech of the convention, examining the frameworks and linguistic devices that made it so effective.

Introduction

At the 2016 Democratic National Convention (DNC) in Philadelphia, the First Lady Michelle Obama showed her support for the Presidential Nominee Hillary Clinton by delivering what was described by the Washington Post as "the best speech of the Democratic National Convention" (Cillizza, 2016). The speech engaged her audience's knowledge of current world events by drawing parallels between current events and historical themes such as female emancipation and racial progress. Michelle Obama’s confidence and fluency has shown a strong sense of identity and her spontaneity demonstrated her skill to interact with her audience, which prove the qualities of a true leader (Charteris-Black, 2014). Although she was there to support Hillary Clinton, in the 2008 election she was at the very same spot supporting her husband who was running against Hillary Clinton. The historian Doris Kearns Goodwin (2016) reminded President Obama in ‘the ultimate exit interview’ of Vanity Fair, that the 2008 election was one of the most contentious and memorable elections of our time which opened doors for candidates of colour and women in future elections. Hence, Hillary Clinton tried to run for President for a second time, but this time with the support of President Obama. To have the support of the first President of colour seemed to be the perfect campaign for another historic election: a woman as the President of the United States. This essay will use Rhetorical Analysis and Critical Metaphor Analysis (CMA) to try to reveal how Michelle Obama made her speech rhetorical and answer the following questions: What rhetorical devices and patterns does Michelle Obama use to make her speech rhetorical? How does she promote Hilary Clinton by using metaphor in combination with other rhetorical devices? To fully frame the questions posed, relevant frameworks will be presented in chapter 2. Chapter 3 will present a rationale for the paragraphs that will be analysed in chapter 4.

Frameworks

Rhetoric is the art of persuasion, politicians use rhetorical devices to appeal to, or to persuade their audiences, a rhetoric is successful only when the audience is persuaded (Charteris-Black, 2014). A rhetorical analysis dissects the written or spoken text into parts and explains how certain parts of speech work together in order to create certain effects the author or speaker wants to achieve to persuade their audience. This chapter will focus on the rhetorical devices that will be used to analyse the speech in chapter 4. Rhetorical Devices Charteris-Black (2011) discusses the overlapping of different strategies that create a powerful interaction which causes a persuasive political communication, involving the composition of the words and sentences chosen by the orator to persuade their audience. To analyse the speech in as much detail as possible, I will provide clear exemplifications of figurative language: rhetorical schemes and rhetorical tropes. A scheme is a figure of speech that affects grammatical structure and contributes to rhythm, balance and timing, these structures are concerned with syntactical change (Charteris-Black, 2011). A trope is a figure of speech which influences the audience to direct attention towards other possible meanings of a word, rather than the literal meaning of a word (Charteris-Black, 2011). Schemes and tropes have an impact on the actual meaning of what is said to cause a rhetorical effect. The difference between the two is: schemes need to be analysed by looking at the grammatical construction of the syntactic patterns, and tropes can be analysed by using lexical semantics because they are more concerned with the complete set of all possible words (Charteris-Black, 2011:288). Aristotle emphasised three kinds of appeals that are the foundation of spoken word: the personal character of the orator (ethos); the importance of making appeals based on emotion by putting the audience into a certain frame of mind (pathos); the proof provided by the words themselves (logos). These appeals are an attempt to earn the audience’s approval by playing to the pathetic, the ethical, and the logical experiences. Aristotle argued that thought could cause emotion, therefore emotion could reciprocate by contributing to an intelligent response (Charteris-Black, 2014:15).

Metaphor

Political speakers often use metaphor which are classical tropes to evoke certain emotions. Charteris-Black (2014:39) suggests that ideology, myth and metaphor are similar as they share a common discourse function of persuasion, which triggers the cognitive and emotional response which can be conscious or unconscious arousal. Hence, it could be suggested that metaphors are used in politics to transfer a set of culturally based psychological associations and beliefs, by causing the audience to think about them in a new way, instead of introducing new ideas. Persuasion could be seen as manipulating existing beliefs, attitudes and values, rather than introducing new ones (Charteris-Black, 2005). Another common purposes of metaphor is to explain complex or unfamiliar topics in a language that is more easily understood by the listener. Charteris-Black (2014) specifies four stages for CMA: Contextual Analysis Metaphor identification Metaphor interpretation Metaphor explanation Charteris-Black (2014) emphasises that the individual psycholinguistic variation of images and associations aroused by metaphor solely depends on personal experiences and argues that metaphors can be related to social cognition. This suggests that the mental projection or resulting image of a metaphor can differ from one person to another based on their social background and life experiences, for example: using the words “slave”, “freedom”, or “liberation” will most likely arouse entirely different emotional patterns within the black community versus the white community within the United States, due to their contrasting historical exposure to issues connected with these terms. Even people in Europe can recall the emotions attached to these words due to films, books and other media, as we share the same etymological source. Approaches to classification of metaphors are: source-based approach which uses basic and literal meanings of words (i.e. dictionary meaning), and the topic-based approach which is more interested in what the metaphor is actually about (i.e. relations with other words close to the metaphor) (Charteris-Black, 2014). In political speeches, there are certain metaphors that seem to return, because these metaphors represent the same meaning and understanding amongst people. Charteris-Black (2011) demonstrates how politicians have used the word “beacon” as a metaphor and how it can activate and integrate a range of schemata. The words close to the metaphor or two sets of metaphors can create different patterns of schemata, which can evoke different ideas in the mind. This relates to lexical cohesion, which can be identified as repetition of words, semantic relations that can arise from synonyms, antonyms, hyponyms or superordinates and collocations which are words that often occur together (Charteris-Black, 2011:67). These ideas will be explored in detail in chapter 4 (see figures 3,4 and 5).

Rationale

The reason this speech was presented as ‘the best speech of the DNC’ is because of this paragraph (Cillizza, 2016): “That is the story of this country. The story that has brought me to the stage tonight. The story of generations of people who felt the lash of bondage, the shame of servitude, the sting of segregation, who kept on striving, and hoping, and doing what needed to be done. So that today, I wake up every morning in a house that was built by slaves. And I watch my daughters - two beautiful intelligent black young women - play with the dog on the White House lawn. And because of Hillary Clinton, my daughters – and all our sons and daughters -now take for granted that a woman can be President of the United States. (Applause.)” This paragraph uses metaphor to influence the listener and create parallels between racial and female emancipation. These themes are then tied directly to Hillary Clinton. Michelle Obama demonstrates that most people do not live in this post-racial and gender equal society and the struggle is still visible for people of colour and women. These topics are highly relevant to many women and men because they were treated differently due to their gender and/ or ethnicity. Michelle Obama’s persuasion is effective due to her ability to mirror her emotions, life experiences, and what she stands for to her audience. Michelle Obama (2015) launched “Let Girls Learn”, a U.S. government initiative aimed at helping adolescent girls achieve a quality education that empowers them to reach their full potential. Her aims to awaken racial progress started as an undergraduate when she wrote her thesis “Princeton-Educated Black and The Black Community” in 1985. This essay will reveal the ‘what’, ‘why’ and ‘how’ that made the speech effective and persuasive, by primarily focussing on the first paragraph and the paragraph presented above. These two paragraphs contain all the devices that Michelle Obama needs to make her speech rhetorical.

4.Speech Analysis

This chapter will focus on Michelle Obama’s speech that took place at the 2016 DNC in Philadelphia. The speech has a readability score of 8.5, which means the sentences are short, the words are simple and it’s written in 7th grade (US) conversational English, which makes it easy to deliver (Readability-Score.com, 2011). The structure of this speech resembles a Matryoshka doll, her entire speech uses the same pattern of devices, the same pattern occurs in one paragraph, and again in one sentence. Reiteration is described as an unconscious process of pattern recognition. This structure appears to be her preferred style as many of her speeches demonstrate this structure: framing a theme or issue then reiterating it again and again to drive home the message (Eidenmuller, 2008). The speech is ethos and pathos focused as she tells personalised stories about her husband, the democratic nominee Hillary Clinton, and her daughters, which is one of the rhetorical tools to make an emotional connection with the audience. Pathos demonstrates how emotions can be aroused by evoking pain, fear, love or humour (Charteris-Black, 2014). All human beings respond to these emotions the same way, due to experiences gained throughout their lives, regardless of language and culture (Johnson, 2015). Michelle Obama opens her speech with an appeal to ethos. Ethos contributes to her ethical credibility by convincing her audience that she is reliable and trustworthy which is equivalent to the ‘goodwill’ of the speaker (Charteris-Black, 2014:9). The cornerstone of a good political speech is trust in the orator. Martin Luther King was known for using ethos and pathos focused speeches rather than logos. Michelle Obama seems to follow these same patterns. In the opening paragraph, Michelle Obama is building trust in herself by demonstrating that she supported Barack in 2008 and that decision turned out to be the correct one. She is inferring that people should look at her past and trust her again with this speech about Hillary Clinton. Michelle Obama is cementing her own credibility and trustworthiness, she is also implying that Hillary Clinton will be able to carry her husband’s torch, because she is there giving a speech to support Hillary after eight years of supporting her husband. "It is hard to believe that it has been eight years since I first came to this convention to talk with you about why I thought my husband should be president. Remember how I told you about his character and his conviction? His decency and grace? The traits we have seen every day as he served our country in the White House." Her opening paragraph sets the tone of her speech by triggering a set of schemata that fit the memories of the audience, it creates a recall of the past eight years, which includes her husband being elected twice to be the President of the United States, this is the start of triggering trust and goodwill by “telling the right story” (Charteris-Black, 2014:98). In this paragraph, she uses pronouns in the first, second, and third person. By using pronouns in the first person, she creates personal appeal. Second person pronoun usage makes the sentence conversational with the audience and her word choice: ‘I came to talk with you’ and not ‘to you’ or ‘at you’ sets the tone for the rest of her speech. Third person directs focus towards her husband: “Remember how I told you about his character and his conviction? His decency and grace?” Tricolon is a scheme that contains three equal and syntactical balanced parts, which are visible in italics. When the audience hears these forms of short phrases it is more likely to remember the information. This is because having three entities combines timing, rhythm and intensity with having the smallest amount of information to create a pattern (Charteris-Black, 2014). The rise and fall in her speech tone indicates “sounding right”, and the facial expressions and gestures contribute to “looking right”, which are the semiotic modes of making an appeal (Charteris-Black, 2014:96). The appearance of Michelle Obama contributes to “looking right”: she is well dressed, her hair is styled, and her skin is smooth which contribute to her overall appeal. “The traits we have seen every day as he served our country in the White House" With this sentence, Michelle is confirming her credibility. She is inferring that people should look at her past and trust her again with this speech about Hillary. This sentence makes use of collective pronouns such as we and our which creates collective power and unity. With the use of the we pronoun, Michelle is including the audience in the speech, building empathy, creating a set of shared values and a sense of unity. Michelle wants to present a vision that both she and the audience are in this together. We also reinforces the connection she makes with our country. Michelle Obama aligns herself with Hillary Clinton, as mothers who care -above all- for our children, this demonstrates “thinking right” (Charteris-Black, 2014:97). Michelle Obama uses pathos many times throughout the speech to create a solid unity with the audience by telling real stories in combination with imagery to make the point that her family is similar to everyone else’s. The next paragraph will illustrate the effects of schemes and tropes, with an appeal to pathos and ethos: "That is the story of this country. The story that has brought me to the stage tonight. And because of Hillary Clinton, my daughters –- and all our sons and daughters -– now take for granted that a woman can be President of the United States (Applause.)” Anaphoric tricolons are the deliberate use of repetition at the beginning and of sentences, and are a device commonly used by politicians to emphasise a theme that they wish to focus on in their speeches (Charteris-Black, 2014). Repetition helps to increase the persuasiveness of the key ideas within the speech. To increase rhetorical effect and make the speech memorable, she starts telling the story strategically by using anaphora, where she repeats the story three times which causes a rhythmic effect that has a slow start that accelerates towards the climax. The repetition of formulaic phrases with various intervals signals to the audience the opportunities at which participation is actively encouraged (Charteris-Black, 2014:42). It makes the words easy on the ear and easy to remember. The first story: “That is the story of this country.” Anaphoric reference occurs when the audience understand a word by referring back to something that has been said before. In the previous paragraph of the speech (see appendix 1) she describes Tim Kaine and Hillary Clinton as leaders who have worked hard to make a difference towards closing the gap of racial segregation, and that the country has come so far to establish a form of unity. The second story: “The story that has brought me to the stage tonight.” This sentence refers back to the first story where it is implied that Michelle Obama is there on that stage to support Hillary Clinton and not Donald Trump, because of her own background and ideologies. Throughout her speech, she does not use his name but it is implied that everything that Hillary Clinton has worked for to form a unity in the country might fall apart, if Donald wins the election. Thus, she is there to make sure Hilary Clinton wins this election. The third story shows combination of use of rhetorical devices: “The story of generations of people who felt the lash of bondage, the shame of servitude, the sting of segregation, who kept on striving, and hoping, and doing what needed to be done.”(Applause) Charteris-Black (2014:202) illustrates ‘sound bites’ (also known as parallelisms), which are the use of pairs of clauses in which the syntax and lexis are matched to produce short, memorable and quotable phrases (which are pointed out in italics). The use of the metaphor with parallelism creates a well-balanced, rhythmic and a syntactical symmetry (which are underlined) that contributes to “sounding right” (Charteris-Black, 2011:104). At the same time, it allows more time for the semantic content to be understood (“the lash of bondage, the shame of servitude, the sting of segregation, who kept on striving, and hoping, and doing what needed to be done.”). Syntactic parallelism enhances the impact of the metaphor because it is a form of hyperbole (Charteris-Black, 2011). Michelle Obama uses the schemes antithesis and alliteration in combination with three examples of empathetic words which are metaphors that semantically relate and arouse emotions of fear and courage that are connected to unify the audience towards a common idea (lash, shame, sting striving, hoping, doing what needed to be done). Antithesis is a device where two contrasting or opposite ideas or words are deliberately placed next to each other to create semantic relations in the mind (see figure 3) (Charteris-Black, 2004). These antitheses themselves occur in triplet and are juxtaposed with a tricolon, which is linguistically described as syntactic repetition. It emphasises one of the two ideas to make her point memorable. Alliteration is the occurrence of the same letter or sound at the beginning of closely connected words (underlined in the text above) and is often used in poetry and by politicians to create a certain flow in a sentence, and it intensifies the message that comes afterwards due to the rhythm (Charteris-Black, 2004). The speech is available on Youtube (PBS NewsHour, 2016), it is noticeable that the alliteration in the second half of the sentence causes a rhythmic effect which intensifies the message and causes the audience to respond with cheers, applause, and whistles. The emotional connection of the words is a clear appeal to pathos. The antithesis and alliteration in the first part triggers the domain and semantic relations in the lexicon by creating images of the brutality inflicted on slaves. She puts two words together that form a collocation (typical form of metaphors in a political rhetoric), and puts metaphors together with the same emotional value that merges schemas and causes these sources to multiply the arousal and rhetorical effect (Charteris-Black, 2014). These metaphors can be categorised as conventional metaphors, as the process shifts between comparison and categorisation. Conventional metaphors may be extended in novel ways, the unity in metaphor between what is seen as conventional and what is perceived as new is a novel metaphor. For example, when the word "bondage" is said, the brain automatically and unconsciously associates other related words (synonyms) and their opposites (antonyms). This is highly individualised and based on the listener’s vocabulary, but in general there will be a set of related words and opposing words in the mind of the listener. By using the positive form (antonym) immediately after the original negative collocations, Michelle Obama forms contrasts. The effect of this contrast is to amplify the strength of the positive emotional arousal after arousing fear. In simple terms, an orator using the word "freedom" immediately after a sentence about "slavery" will have a much stronger mental impact than if the orator had simply mentioned "freedom" in isolation. The intended purpose of this sentence is to again imply: if anyone other than Hillary Clinton wins the election, considerable segregation problems may occur. The audience responds with a thunder of cheer, applause, and audibility, due to empathy towards the First Lady. Thus, the sentence could be "... a house built by bondage, shame, servitude...". These images are not just emotionally confronted by the previous sentence and history, but also recent tension created by the media (i.e. black lives matter, Beyoncé’s video which says ‘stop shooting us’). Michelle Obama is inferring that her ancestors possibly have been slaves without being explicit. The semantic tension created in this part of the speech was highly effective in creating coherence and a persuasive political narrative. Michelle Obama then ends this section on a positive note with a sentence referring to her main theme (the future): ”and I watch my daughters –- two beautiful, intelligent, black young women –- playing with their dogs on the White House lawn. (Applause.)” This part of the speech shows understanding of all of what Michelle Obama personally has achieved just by standing where she is; her personal American dream, her African American Dream, the dream that symbolises and reminds most people of Martin Luther King (“I have a dream”). This part is a clear appeal to ethos. The audience cheer again because she has created a co-constructed speech event. This sentence signifies a shift in the rhetoric from race to gender by bringing together the two elements and explicitly pointing out that her daughters are black young women. These two groups have gone through similar processes of emancipation in the past. The White House symbolises the American dream and by associating her daughters with the American dream, in the context of their home, then contrasting this with the origins of the White House (slavery), she is suggesting that if it is possible to come on this journey from slavery to the White House, then it surely is possible for women to have that equal journey. This suggestion is made in the following transition: “And because of Hillary Clinton, my daughters –- and all our sons and daughters -– now take for granted that a woman can be President of the United States. (Applause.)” The paragraph has no relation to Hillary Clinton and yet it sounds right because of the shift of the rhetoric from race to gender. The paragraph as a whole shows how coherence and cohesion work in discourse. The cohesion gives the impression that the paragraph is unified with the linguistic means, whereas the coherence has caused different types of mental processes (Charteris-Black, 2005:55). This implicitly causes interaction between Michelle Obama and her audience at the end of the sentence which gives the speech momentum and a feeling of shared purpose and unity. Michelle Obama repeats daughters three times in this paragraph and twice in this sentence alone. She is re-initiating her theme which is the future of our children and our country and that people must vote for an equal future where a woman can be President, thus people should vote for Hillary Clinton. Michelle Obama has presented this paragraph with a full package of devices and this analysis has shown: how, what, and why she used the devices to get the response she wanted from the audience.

Conclusion

This speech analysis demonstrates the rhetorical devices that Michelle Obama has used to make her speech rhetorical. Michelle Obama’s persuasion is effective due to her ability to mirror her emotions and life experiences to her audience. The approval of the audience is obtained by primarily focussing on ethical (ethos) and emotional (pathos) devices. The two paragraphs analysed contain all the devices that Michelle Obama needs to make her speech rhetorical. The first paragraph is entirely ethos-based where she works to build her credibility by creating unity with the audience, building a foundation for her theme, and aligning herself with Hillary Clinton. The second paragraph focusses on creating an emotional connection with the audience and thus is heavily pathos-based. Michelle Obama chooses simple words and syntax that highlight and intensify the sentences, where she chokes and is moved to tears. Her background and interests mean she is able to deliver this speech persuasively, with authenticity and from the heart. As an undergraduate she wrote "to them, I will always be black first and a student second" (Obama, 1985:2) which shows her ideological motivation of language use in her speech. Schemes contribute to her rhythm, balance, and timing in this paragraph which amplify the emotional impact of the speech. The rise and fall in her speech tone, facial expressions and gestures are an indication of her emotional involvement with the contents of the paragraph, her aim is for the audience to be similarly involved. She brings it all together: story, repetition, analogy, allusion, matching clauses, contrast, alliteration, anaphora, tricolons, chiasmus, simple and emotional word choices, and a persuasive delivery. Throughout the speech, Michelle Obama uses a wide range of rhetorical devices to present herself as an authority on the subject of racial and female equality. She continuously uses ethos and pathos based language with combinations of rhetorical devices in order to maximise the impact of her words to drive home her theme: the future in which Hillary Clinton is President of the USA.
