Are Proficient Language Learners Able to Identify Different Types of Corrective Feedback, and What Are Their Corrective Feedback Preferences?

Abstract

A mini-research project asking whether proficient language learners can recognise different types of corrective feedback, which types they notice, and which they prefer - filling a gap in a literature that has mostly studied lower-level learners.

Introduction

Motivation

Second language processes, the production of language, and Corrective Feedback (CF) have been under debate for decades as many researchers have investigated how to improve learners’ language skills (Ellis & Shintani, 2014). CF helps learners to notice their errors and what they intend to produce. It stimulates learners to test their theories about how the target language (TL) functions, and it reinforces learners to reflect on the language they do not fully grasp (Vold, 2022). This results in awareness about the TL and pushes learners to self-correct and use the target language more proficiently. However, most Second Language Acquisition (SLA) in English as a Second Language (ESL) research investigates: low-level/ intermediate second language (L2) learners exam courses (e.g., IELTS), which are usually pursued by future university students (Gholami, 2021) high school students in one country, providing a monoculture with various language levels (Vold, 2022)

In addition, most studies employ university students to participate in their experiments, which excludes a large part of the population. To avoid these biases, this research solely focuses on high-level learners from a language school where the learners have different reasons for studying ESL that are more in keeping with real-life situations, which will be a broader sample of the population than the student populations that are often researched.

1.2 Previous studies

Vold (2022) suggests CF has two components: Input-providing vs. output-prompting strategies Implicit vs. explicit strategies Input-providing strategies provide learners with the correct form of the TL through:

Explicit correction, where the teacher points out the error/gap the learners which push learners to self-correct. Recasts, come in different forms (i.e., implicit and explicit strategies) reformulating the correct forms back to the learners. Explicit strategies are usually noticed by learners, as they are a direct form of correction through clear repetition, whereas implicit forms may not be easily understood as they happen in conversation. This approach could heavily rely on the rapport between the learners and their teacher as it depends on the turn-taking process between them (Ellis & Shintani, 2014). Metalinguistic comments provide technical terms of language which can be complicated as students may not know the metalinguistic language, causing frustration (Ellis & Shintani, 2014). However, correction can also occur in conversation with peers or teachers, and be meaning-focused or used to resolve errors through negotiation. Conversational recasts are linked to implicit strategies as they can occur spontaneously and be repeated in context. Didactic recasts are linked to explicit strategies as they can occur in repetition and clarification requests (Vold, 2022). Output-prompting strategies push learners to self-correct through clarification requests or repetitions, which are considered implicit strategies. This suggests that learners may not pick up on implicit strategies consciously (Vold, 2022).

1.3 Aims of research

This study aims to identify if learners are aware of the different types of feedback in the classroom, which types they notice, and prefer. CF research enhances the field’s interpretation of interactional patterns which can occur in ESL classrooms, but it rarely offers a broader picture of what the proficient learners want in forms of CF, as most research focuses on the other proficiency levels or courses. Mitchell & Myles (2004) argue the way CF is used is crucial in language development as it could lead to a negative learning experience. This suggests the importance of how CF is delivered to create meaningful and purposeful learning experiences, which is why it is important to understand how students feel while being corrected.

1.4 Research Questions

How many types of CF do proficient (C2) learners notice and/or recognise in the classroom? (a)Which types of CF do proficient (C2) learners prefer and (b)what are their reasons? In order to answer the questions posed, the methodology (2), results and discussion (3), and conclusion (4) will be presented in the following sections.

2. Methodology

2.1 Context and Participants

The participants are studying English at Education First (EF) International Language Centres. They are proficient (C2) learners according to the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) for Languages. The results of this study will provide data from random sampling (Dörnyei& Csizér, 2011), which involves language learners with different L1 backgrounds (i.e., Belgian, Brazilian, French, German, Japanese, Dutch, and Swiss) who have passed the C1 test at EF in order to be in a C2 classroom. These students change teachers and are tested every six weeks to show their progression to be able to enter the next level, which indicates that these learners are at the same language level based on their reading, writing, speaking, and listening skills. Ensuring all participants have the same language level will result in reliable data (Blaxter, 2010). These students (6 female, 7 male) are in Brisbane, Australia, traveling and studying English for different reasons (e.g., migration, marriage, study, travel). These differences in interest suggest different motivation levels to learn a language, which resembles real word variations (Dörnyei, 1998).

2.2 Instruments and Data Collection

Quantitative and qualitative data will be gathered through a survey employing an adapted version of Vold’s research (2022): 5-point Likert scale to ensure that the learners can show a certain degree of preference in CF and also have the option to point out if they do not remember receiving a type of CF, as they may have heard it but not retained it. Open-ended questions to allow the learners to explain why they prefer a certain type of CF. This will provide input to teachers on why certain types of CF are preferred or disliked. To ensure students complete the survey, it is important to employ students who are willing to do the work, and avoid multiple tasks as students could get bored, hungry, and/ or annoyed (Mackey, 2020). To avoid result bias, the students received a link for the survey by the Director of Studies at the end of their six-week study at C2.1. The students were also notified they would get a new teacher after the break. This prevents them from feeling obliged to participate to please their current teacher and it encourages them to be honest in their responses. To gather the data in a controlled environment, the participants were asked to complete the survey in the school’s library using Chromebooks to ensure everyone participates equally to gather reliable data (Blaxter, 2010)

Data Analysis

The data will be analysed per question and the results will be used to answer the research questions posed.

Questions 6 and 7 are used to prime the students for the survey and make them aware of their contribution to their learning as explicit learning becomes implicit knowledge when students proactively contribute to their learning progress (VanPatten, 2015).

Question 8 employs a variation of questions to raise awareness of the teacher’s pedagogic methods, as the learning process of students is a two-way street which is informed by a turn-taking processes, delivery of relevant topics, and materials to generate interest (Vold, 2022).

Question 9 introduces the CF students have received in class. The teacher explained the forms of feedback weeks prior to the survey. This was to ensure the learners could identify the different types of CF they received from their teacher to answer research question 1. Recasts were explained as CF by the teacher by repeating correct forms implicitly and explicitly. These were combined and targeted toward student understanding. This served to minimise frustration and confusion when completing the survey. (Dörnyei & Csizér, 2011).

Question 10 is posed to answer research question 2a, which can only be answered if the learners can identify the types of CF to answer research question 1. Here they can show their preferences by choosing what type of CF they like and dislike. Only the ‘Strongly Agree’ and ‘Agree’ results will be used to measure the types of preferred CF.

Question 11 makes learners think in detail about the CF they received. This will support the data gathered from question 10 by justifying their answers and giving reasons why they think that different types of CF are important to their learning. This question also gives room for learners who may have noticed other types of CF which were not mentioned in the survey. Question 12 gives learners the opportunity to share any negative experiences with regard to CF. This information would give the teacher some insight into adjusting the delivery of CF. In addition, it could give some useful insight for future research on why learners dislike a certain type of CF. Question 13 pushes learners in a different way, to be honest about their learning experience and hopefully will highlight the value of CF without prompting the students. Question 14 gives the learners a chance to give their opinions about the course and how they would improve it. This gives individual data on what learners thought they missed in the course or what satisfied them during their learning experiences. Questions 11-14 will give qualitative data from each individual, which will serve to answer Research Questions 2 (a) and (b).

3. Results and Discussion

3.1 Results

Only 15 students completed the survey - the results from B2 and C1 students were excluded from the data analysis as the focus of this study is on C2 students.

The students either ‘Agree’ or ‘Strongly Agree’ to the Skill and Responsiveness of their teacher, which implies the teacher employs various effective materials, has a good rapport with their students, and gives CF in their teaching. This validates the reliability of the data in the following questions.

Question 9, overall, the majority of the students seem to ‘Agree’ or ‘Strongly Agree’ in identifying the different types of CF (i.e., Recasts, Oral Feedback, and Written Feedback). However, 30.8% of the participants do not remember receiving metalinguistic feedback whereas 69.2% do agree with receiving it. This could suggest that almost a third of the students were not paying attention to the lessons with explicit metalinguistic feedback or they could not uptake the correct form as they did not know/ understand the metalinguistic rule. These students could be the same third who replied regarding their effort and contribution to learning in questions 6&7 with ‘Fair’ and ‘Satisfactory’ as per below.

Question 10, in which participants were asked which type of CF they enjoyed the most, 92.3% of students appreciate receiving CF daily in different forms of recasts. 92.3% also responded to enjoying Written CF. 84.6% value CF on their speaking skills, where learners deliver a presentation, and the teacher writes down the oral CF. This suggests that C2 learners are more concerned with correcting their written language as they have achieved communicative competence. One student (7.7%) shows dislike for receiving CF on their speaking.

Question 11, according to the qualitative data, learners highly value their weekly feedback on their speaking, and especially their writing. Students do not seem to have negative experiences receiving explicit feedback as 53.8% of the learners mention the value of being corrected in real time. In addition, learners identify ‘repeating the right form’ solidifies the learner’s preferences in CF. Here, the learners justify the answers they have given in question 10, which answers research questions 1 and 2.

This section has fewer responses than the previous one. This may suggest any of the following: that learners prefer to give feedback on the things they recognise easily, that there is nothing to add, that they do not trust the anonymous survey, that they do not want to give negative feedback out of fear, or the question was not understood correctly (Dörnyei & Csizér, 2011). According to the results, the students appreciate and value different types of CF in their classrooms.

When generalising the course in its entirety for six weeks with one teacher, 30.8% of the learners still mention CF as the most useful and valuable in their course. Students also mention interaction with other students in the class being very important, as it plays a significant role in SLA and stimulates learners to notice and correct their errors. Interaction causes learners to negotiate for meaning which is an effective way of language learning between peers, as it enables them to notice their gaps, providing comprehensible input through negotiation (Ellis & Shintani, 2014).

3.2 Discussion

How many types of CF do proficient (C2) learners notice and/or recognise in the classroom?

Overall, the learners were able to identify the different types of CF in the quantitative data and elaborate on them in the qualitative data, which means proficient language learners are able to notice CF in their classroom and learn by self-correction, similar to previous studies (Vold, 2022). However, how much CF they actually notice is hard to measure in this data as it remains unclear on the actual uptake of the CF noticed. The data shows that some students do not recall receiving CF. 2.(a)Which types of CF do proficient (C2) learners prefer and (b)what are their reasons?

The data clearly demonstrates that C2 learners prefer CF in the form of recasts on their speaking skills, and oral and written CF on their writing skills based on the summary of the quantitative data as shown above. The results from the qualitative data show that learners value real-time CF as it helps them to become aware of their errors. The students also mention the weekly feedback they receive from their teacher on their spoken and written output. Even though all types of feedback were appreciated, the CF given to their writing was of the highest value, according to the qualitative data. Existing research suggests that direct feedback can be received negatively by students, as they could feel embarrassed in front of their peers (Mitchell & Myles, 2004). In contrast, the data from this study indicate that learners appreciate all forms of feedback they receive from their teacher, as it promotes language development. It could also suggest that proficient learners have reached the final stage of a language course and may not want to pursue further studies, as they have reached the maximum level according to the CEFR. This course of action could result in wanting to know all the errors they are still making in order to fix those gaps.

4. Conclusion

The aim of this study was to investigate if proficient ESL learners can identify and show awareness of different types of feedback in their classroom. The results show that they notice CF, which in turn notifies them of their errors, leading to self-correction. As previous research targeted low-level L2 learners, exam courses, university students, and mainly monocultures, this study was able to provide insights into proficient language learners and their preferences in CF. This study contributes to and helps advance classroom interaction research and the CF field’s understanding of interactional patterns which can occur in ESL classrooms, and offers a broader picture of what learners actually want in forms of CF. This mini-research, unfortunately, had some limitations. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small as not all students wanted to participate in the survey. Secondly, it would have been of high value to compare the students’ preferences by recording them during a speaking or writing test to measure if there were improvements on prior made errors. This would take the research a step further as the classes would need to be recorded to capture the CF, to then link the errors to specific learners, and follow their progress throughout their course. Finally, to answer the research questions in more depth, the research should have contained examples of CF where the learners would identify the types of CF to prime them for the questions posed in this research, which would verify their responses. To avoid the students getting bored or frustrated as the literature suggested, the survey was kept short. However, this does open doors for new studies in the future, as it would be relevant to repeat this study at EF Brisbane across all levels (i.e., CEFR A1-C2). This would compare the responses of students at various levels at one language school by correcting the limitations suggested in this study.
