How and Why Is Acquiring a Second Language (as an Adult) Unlike Acquiring a First Language?

Abstract

A comparison of first and second language acquisition in adults - from the very different “initial state” of the learner to the influence of the first language - and a reflection on how studying SLA theory changed the author’s view of how second languages are learned.

First language (L1) learners start their language journeys before they are born. To them, the starting point of their language journey, which can be referred to as the “initial state”, starts from the developmental phase (Gass et al, 2020). L1 learners have to start from nothing by learning the forms and functions of their L1. In contrast, second language (L2) learners already know how to communicate and are aware of language functions, from their L1. Their “initial state” is entirely different. Therefore, to carry out the pragmatic functions and meaning of the L2, learners must only acquire a new set of forms of the L2. They use the prior knowledge from their L1. Gass et al (2020) argue that the relatedness of the L1 in relation to the L2 plays a big role in acquiring an L2, suggesting languages in the same language families are easier to acquire. For example, given an L1 of English, learning a second Germanic language is easier than learning a non-Germanic language (e.g., Japanese). In the latter case, the learner may fail to acquire the language, this could demotivate learners. Understanding motivation is important as everyone has an L1 but learning an L2 is often a conscious choice. Learners will have different motives and motivations for acquiring an L2. This affects their drive and determination to acquire a language, whereas L1 learners manage to acquire their L1 regardless of which language they initially encounter when/before they are born.

To what extent can SLA be considered either an innate or emergent process? Language learning occurs through social interaction as it is all around us. Through interaction, learners can pick up information on frequency, salience, prototypicality, and contingency, this is how we process and learn a language. Interaction is fundamental in language learning and human communication, however, interaction is not just socialising, it allows us to express our thoughts, and indicate the workings of our internal world. There are times when people communicate without using words, humans also interact through eye contact, hand gestures, and body language. Schmidt (1990) argues that L2 learners learn through noticing, which means they need to notice the gap between their current abilities and usage of the L2, and the proficiency that they aim to attain. For example, if the learner does not get corrected, that person will not notice the error and continue using the wrong forms. Through interaction and negotiation with the learners, reformulating the error turns it into interactionally modified input (Lyster & Saito, 2010). This occurs when the learner is stimulated to experiment with the new language and adapt the correct forms and structures in the L2. This development is essential in their language progression because it offers circumstances to reflect on their mistakes. Through discussion, learners can analyse their language problems implicitly and explicitly. Humans have the innate ability to learn and turn explicit knowledge into implicit knowledge, which is what makes SLA an emergent process through interaction.

What do you consider ‘ultimate attainment’ of SLA in terms of L2 development, and to what extent do you believe such attainment to be possible?

Personally, the term ‘ultimate attainment’ is a very strong expression and impossible to achieve for both L2 and L1 learners. I work and meet with native speakers, who also make mistakes in their L1. Instead of setting the goal to ultimate attainment, it should be phrased as ‘ultimate success’. This does not lower expectations for learners, but rather empowers them on the road to achieving a suitable level of communicative competence for their situation and requirements. Setting lofty expectations for attaining absolute fluency may discourage learners. Ellis and Wulff (2015) argue that language learning is mainly based on the learner’s exposure to the language. In order to achieve native-like speech, it is important to work and socialise with native speakers in order to pick up on language forms and functions learners would not acquire in a classroom setting. For example, if the learner is studying English and is living in Australia, they are exposed to the L2 at all times. The learner hears and stores utterances implicitly and explicitly, which fuels the usage-based account as frequency is key. For instance, I was exposed to English daily and even as a proficient speaker, would hear new language I did not understand when I lived in England (e.g., I’m so knackered). After I understood the meaning, different forms and structures of the phrases appeared around me and I found myself beginning to adopt and use them naturally in my speech. Showing progression in language learning and overcoming obstacles which adult learners face should be the ultimate goal.

To what extent is SLA a social rather than an individual process?

Language learning is defined by communicative competence, which reinforces the ability to use language in communication (Saville-Troike,1988). This term suggests that SLA is a social and interactive process. It is not enough to solely know the forms of a language but also to achieve sociolinguistic competence to interpret meaning in social settings. For example, in Chinese culture, it is custom to self-deprecate when presenting a meal. It is then expected to praise the person who cooked, but if someone does not know how to act in this social setting, not only do they fail in their sociolinguistic competence, but also their strategic competence as they cannot repair the communication breakdown. This is an example of how different competencies interact with one another. Vygotsky’s learning theory illustrates that even though learning starts from the individual, it only takes place through interaction and negotiation for meaning. In SLA, scaffolding is a technique to deliver a variety of instructions to support the students to understand the content. This supports learners to gain a better understanding of the subject taught and their learning progress (Vygotsky, 1978). The aim of scaffolding is to avoid frustration, intimidation, negative emotions, or being discouraged when the learners have to complete a task in class (Vygotsky, 1978). Learners need to notice their language gaps, which they would not be able to achieve without assistance. Scaffolding makes the task easier, increases interest, maintains the lesson aims, and creates a positive learning environment for all learners. This helps the learner's language process and progress, which makes SLA a social process as learners would not be able to achieve this on their own. To what extent do all L2 learners have the same experiences during the SLA process? Everyone who learns or is learning a second language has a reason for why they are learning that language (Dörnyei, 1998). Younger learners have the advantage of learning languages easily. However, it depends on the learner’s language input and motivation. Children need a reason to learn and maintain a language. If they are just learning to pass classes in high school, learners may only learn to pass a test and forget everything they learned after graduating if they are not using the language in everyday life. This is due to neurophysiological factors, when neural pathways are not stimulated, there is no reason for the brain to maintain them. Dornyei and Csizer (1998) argue that with the right teaching strategies L2 learners will be motivated to study, participate and learn the L2. In addition, being able to understand what kind of learner they are, will motivate them to use that style more frequently inside and outside the classroom (Reid, 1987). Language learning is personal as everyone learns differently. However, the thing that drives us all regardless of what we are learning is motivation. We need a reason to learn. We could have the same motivation to learn a language and may not learn the same way due to individual differences as mentioned previously. In summary, the only commonality between L2 learners is that they are learning a second language, but the reason why makes them all different.

To what extent is SLA a linear or dynamic process? SLA is a complicated process which changes many neural pathways throughout someone’s learning progress (Larsen-Freeman, 2015). Nothing in SLA is static, it is a continuous process of change as learners progress, which also implies their learning styles and motivation. Learners themselves change over time, their personal experiences influence their language learning, styles, strategies, and maintenance. Larsen-Freeman (2015) argues that with the right input the different systems within the mind get activated which changes many neural pathways (e.g., bilinguals changing their environment and using another language). Castro (2018) argues that L2 motivation is a dynamic process as it is impossible to always stay motivated. This has an impact on language learning as people learn better when they see the progress which drives motivation (Castro, 2018). L2 learners react differently to similar tasks on different occasions, which makes learning a complex system (Gass et al, 2020). It is impossible to generalise and think language learning is linear because there would be a clear start and finish line for everyone, which would make learning a language easy to accomplish. The complexity theory illustrates that individual differences are a process of cause-and-effect, as language itself changes over time. For example, the English language we speak today does not resemble the English language in Shakespearean times. Language evolves making it a dynamic process for all learners including native speakers. 7. With reference to a specific week of SLAT7805 course content, reflect on how knowledge of SLA theory gained on this course has changed your perception of how second languages are learned In week 8, we looked into the effects of L1 transfer, which enlightened and piqued my curiosity, as I had very strong opinions about this matter. Initially, I was of opinion that languages which do not have certain forms (e.g., prepositions) would make it easier for L2 learners to learn, as it would not interfere with the forms of their L1. The reason for this thought process came from my students. For example, my advanced Dutch students were not able to use the correct prepositions because of other prepositions used in Dutch (e.g., ‘in the bus’ instead of ‘on the bus’), whereas my Arabic students, who do not have prepositions in Arabic, were able to learn and use them correctly at intermediate level. However, the lectures and literature in this specific week made me understand that depending on cross-linguistic variance, learners will show different forms of progress. Some learners may experience positive L1 transfer, and progress faster compared to learners who experience negative L1 transfer, which will cause the learner to take longer to progress to the next level (Crosthwaite, 2014). For example, Korean does not have an article system (i.e., a/an, the), this causes learners with this background to take longer to acquire articles compared to learners who do have such quantifying forms in their language (Crosthwaite, 2014). On the contrary, Von Sutterheim et all (2021) argue that regardless of the level and the frequent exposure to the L2 of a learner, their L1 will always interfere. They argue that students are perfectly capable of passing exams in controlled settings, however, the results are very different when learners have to produce language naturally. This is when learners would instinctively revert back to their L1 forms. To investigate this further, I have written a proposal on this subject for my other course. (1465+ 302=1767words)
