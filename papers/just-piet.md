# Just Piet

*Written in 2015, as a student at the Hogeschool van Amsterdam — this is my "one way" essay: the version where the assignment asked me to argue the proponents' side.*

---

Every year, for decades, families have stood waiting in torrential rain for the arrival of Sinterklaas and his Black Pete. This Dutch tradition has brought joy — and sweets — to young and old alike. In the beginning, Black Pete was not a grown man at all: he was a little African child, rescued out of slavery by Sinterklaas, and that is how he became the saint's helper. The division of labour was simple: Sinterklaas brought presents to the children who were good, and Black Pete brought the "stick" for the ones who were bad. As the years passed and the holiday became more of a commercial celebration, comparable to the American way of doing Christmas, Black Pete was promoted from forbidding enforcer to funny sidekick — the silly, joyful character children adore, who hands out sweets to everyone.

Then, a few years ago, this beloved holiday turned into a racial discussion that has been blown out of proportion. The topic became impossible to avoid in the Netherlands; it even drew the attention of other countries, and it divided the nation into two groups — opponents and proponents — with no middle ground in sight. Opponents of Black Pete claim the holiday is racist and intolerant towards a certain group of people in our multicultural society. But while everyone argues about the symbol, the real question is being forgotten: how do we talk to each other about it? It seems this polarisation is causing a huge chasm, simply because of cultural differences — and a lack of trying to understand the other side.

Apparently, the arrival of Sinterklaas and his Black Petes brings pain, frustration and humiliation to a certain group of people — supposedly because of the name and the colour that come with the character. The proponents' answer to that is well known: he looks the way he looks because he climbs through chimneys, and the name simply refers to that. The fact that the character is played by Dutch people wearing face paint, afro wigs and jester's clothes is, to the opponents, a harsh undertone of humiliation; to the proponents, it is costume, not commentary. Emotions rise highest around the children — when a child cannot tell the difference between a regular person of colour and Black Pete, and simply loves him, without meaning any harm. And that is the proponents' strongest feeling of all: the children love Black Pete. Nobody in this debate is asking to take Sinterklaas away. The original request, from before the argument started, was modest: change the name from Black Pete to just Pete, and swap the black face paint for other colours, so that nobody gets hurt. In time, children will not know or remember the difference.

But the proponents ask: what exactly is the harm? Black Pete has been promoted to Sinterklaas' sidekick, and they see that as something positive — as reaching equality for the character. From their perspective, without Black Pete there is no Sinterklaas; children love him for his silly ways, his joyful character, and the sweets he hands out to everyone. They see a character moving forward and being celebrated, not stigmatised. And underneath much of the proponents' frustration sits a harder argument, one that is rarely said out loud in polite company: some feel that people who choose to come and live in this country should adapt to its rules and traditions — just as Dutch people adapt when they move abroad. For example: if you want to work for the Emirates, women have to wear a hat with a veil, otherwise they do not get hired. You adjust, or you go elsewhere.

Both groups, meanwhile, seem to be forgetting the main idea, which is: to make our children happy. Children do not see colour — Pete could be any colour, and he was black for decades without causing any problem. It is the adults who have turned this into a racial issue. Yet even the proponents do not claim the image must stay frozen forever. Traditions do change over time, and nobody seriously denies that this one will — what they ask for is time, and a gentle pace. Change one thing each year, so the transition does not arrive all at once. Last year they removed the golden earrings and the red lipstick, and the sky did not fall. We are human: we find change difficult, however small. Every time we get a new phone, we struggle to adjust to it because we are accustomed to the old one — and then, after a while, it feels as if the new phone was the only phone we ever owned. Traditions, like phones, can change gradually and still remain themselves.

So, to sum up this slow development: the Netherlands is ready to change — but all it asks is time to realise, time to adjust, time to grieve and let go of the Black Pete it has loved for so many years. Unfortunately, the argument has already divided the country in a way nobody originally intended. People have lost sight of the initial purpose, which was to make children happy without hurting anyone's racial background. And on the matter of "ruining traditions": in time, traditions change, just as language, technology, science and fashion change — they do not disappear, whatever one side of the Dutch population fears.

On the whole, it is not about who is an opponent or a proponent of Black Pete. It is about how we react to one another, how we listen to one another, how we approach one another — so that the divisions in our society can heal, and we can celebrate all our traditions together.
