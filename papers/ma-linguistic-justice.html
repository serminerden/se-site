Towards Linguistic Justice: The Politics of Discourse and Language Teaching for Indigenous Empowerment in Australia

Abstract

The silencing of Indigenous voices has been a persistent feature of Australian and global society since colonisation. This essay examines how language teaching can become a powerful force for Indigenous empowerment - through cultural recognition, bilingual education and supportive policy - and advance linguistic justice in Australia.

Introduction

The silencing of Indigenous voices has been a persistent feature of Australian and global society since colonisation, with a long history of denying Indigenous people their cultural practices, beliefs, and languages (Rose, 1991). The impact of this silencing has been profound, with Indigenous people experiencing high levels of social and economic disadvantage, as well as ongoing trauma and intergenerational trauma (Rose, 1991). This topic is highly relevant and related to language teaching because it highlights the importance of being aware of power dynamics and their effects on language use in social relations. It is essential for language teachers to recognise the social and cultural context in which language is used, and to create a classroom environment that is inclusive and empowering for all students. By creating awareness of the way language is used to silence certain groups and perspectives, language teachers can encourage critical thinking and facilitate discussions that challenge dominant narratives and power structures (Bray, 2022; Van Dijk, 2015). Doing so informs the relevant development of intercultural competence, as understanding and navigating different cultural identities and perspectives is a key component of effective intercultural communication. This refers to the ability to communicate and interact effectively with people from diverse cultural backgrounds in general. Furthermore, incorporating Indigenous languages and cultures in language teaching can also challenge and disrupt the dominant monolingual and monocultural norms in Australian society, due to the imposition of English as a dominant language owing to colonisation. It is possible that a more inclusive and diverse approach to language teaching and learning, which can benefit not only Indigenous students but also non-Indigenous students who may come from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds (Hinton, 2017). Thus, this essay will focus on the importance of integrating Indigenous languages and cultures, so that future generations can develop a deeper understanding of Indigenous perspectives and worldviews. As it can enhance intercultural competence and enable communication and effective interaction within Australian society. The literature review will illustrate the history, the importance of remaining languages, and government policies.

Literature Review

The Struggle for Cultural Recognition and Empowerment

Indigenous Australians have been subjected to linguistic and cultural oppression throughout Australia's history, with their languages and cultures being suppressed in various ways, this is a well-established fact in the literature on Indigenous Australian languages and cultures (Bray, 2022; Hinton, 2017). The policies of assimilation and forced removal of children from their families, (known as the Stolen Generations), have had a deleterious impact on the transmission and maintenance of Indigenous languages and cultures (Moreton-Robinson & Walter, 2009). As a result of these policies, many Indigenous people have experienced a loss of connection to their language and culture, leading to a loss of identity and self-esteem (Ford et al., 2014). Hinton (2017) argues that teaching the Navajo language and culture in schools in the United States helped to promote intercultural competence and understanding among students. As stated previously, using a sociocultural lens allows us to understand how language use and discourse are intertwined with broader social structures, power dynamics, and relationships (Bray, 2022; Van Dijk, 2015). Sociocultural theory suggests that learning and development are shaped by social and cultural contexts, and that language use is a key means through which social practices and identities are constructed and negotiated (Vygotsky, 1978). In the case of Indigenous Australians, language use has been the main source of struggle for cultural recognition and empowerment (Ford et al., 2014; Rose, 1991). This informs the concept of language learning as a means of promoting social justice and empowering marginalised communities. For example, by encouraging students to learn and use languages that are traditionally marginalised or stigmatised, such as the vast array of First Nations languages, language teachers can help promote linguistic diversity and challenge linguistic imperialism (Hamid & Jahan, 2015). The concept of linguistic justice arises from the recognition that language is a fundamental aspect of identity and culture, and the denial of the right to use one's own language can have significant negative impacts on an individual or group's well-being (Hinton, 2017). Linguistic justice advocates for the right of individuals or groups to use and maintain their own language and culture without being discriminated against or marginalised. In Australia, the issue of language instruction has been a significant concern, particularly for Indigenous children. The imposition of English as the dominant language has resulted in the marginalisation and loss of Indigenous languages and cultures. This issue has been discussed in the literature, with scholars highlighting the need to recognise and incorporate Indigenous languages and cultures into language curricula (Ford et al., 2014; Hinton, 2017; Moreton-Robinson & Walter, 2009).

The research of Hamid and Jahan (2015) on the language of instruction in Bangladesh provides insights into how the choice of language of instruction can have a significant impact on access to education and socioeconomic opportunities. The aim of this research was to examine the public debates over the medium of instruction in Bangladeshi print media, and it revealed that this choice is far from neutral: it is tied to people's identity and to deep social divides. Students from privileged backgrounds, who have access to better quality education, have significantly more opportunities for socioeconomic mobility than their counterparts from marginalised backgrounds, who often lack proficiency and face linguistic and cultural barriers. In this way, current language policies and practices perpetuate the socioeconomic inequalities between these groups. Similarly, in Australia, language policies and practices have had a profound effect on the education and well-being of Indigenous people (Hinton, 2017). In recent years, scholars have shown a growing interest in promoting linguistic equity for Indigenous people in Australia, as it has been emphasised that recognizing Indigenous languages and cultures as integral parts of Australia's heritage and identity is crucial for achieving linguistic justice (Bray, 2022). A more inclusive language policy that recognises and supports the diversity of languages spoken and the provision of quality education, would show solidarity, respect, and acknowledgment. For example, in years six to eight, Japanese, German, French, Italian, Indonesian, and Chinese (Mandarin) are mandatory in all Australian state schools. However, schools can choose to provide Indigenous languages and Torres Strait Islander languages to fulfill the requirement only on an elective basis (Kohler & Curnow, 2014). The Australian curriculum prioritises Asian languages over Indigenous languages. This is wonderful for Asian students as this will make them feel included and validated in Australian society. As a result, Indigenous students will feel excluded even more in their own country. Receiving recognition is crucial to identity (re)development as the self cannot exist on its own, people need validation for their existence and feel included in society (Benwell & Stokoe, 2006). Teacher training programs should support multilingual education and equip teachers with the skills to work effectively with students from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds, as it is also important to address the socioeconomic inequalities that are perpetuated by current language policies and practices (Hamid & Jahan, 2015).

Language as an Element of Identity and Culture

Boroditsky and Gaby (2010) details how different languages represent time and how these representations may influence cognitive processes. The study examines two Indigenous languages, Kuuk Thaayorre and Pormpuraaw, which use cardinal directions (i.e., north, south, east, west) rather than relative concepts (i.e., left, right, front, back) to describe spatial relationships. The research suggests that the use of cardinal directions in these languages may lead to heightened awareness and understanding of spatial relationships, as well as a different way of thinking about time. Specifically, speakers of these languages appear to have a greater ability to orient themselves in space and to remember past events in relation to their spatial context. The results have important implications for understanding the relationship between language and cognition, as well as the diversity of cognitive processes across different cultures and linguistic groups. Although the study does not specifically address the importance of teaching Indigenous languages, it does provide evidence for the broader importance of language preservation and revitalization efforts. Boroditsky's research highlights the ways in which language can shape our cognition and perception of the world around us. Different languages encode and emphasise different aspects of the environment and culture, which can in turn influence the way people think, reason, and interact with others. For Indigenous people, language is often deeply intertwined with cultural identity and knowledge systems justice (Moreton-Robinson & Walter, 2009), and with the loss of language, described above, there have been profound effects on Indigenous individuals and communities, leading to a weakening or erasure of cultural traditions and knowledge transmission. Therefore, the preservation and revitalisation of Indigenous languages can play a crucial role in supporting cultural continuity and resilience. Advocating the use and teaching of Indigenous languages can help to ensure that these languages and the unique worldviews and knowledge systems they embody are passed down to future generations. Additionally, there is importance and value in recognising Indigenous languages and cultures as integral parts of Australia's heritage. This can be accomplished by incorporating Indigenous languages and cultures into language curricula (Hinton, 2017), which can lead to individuals reclaiming their heritage, strengthening their sense of identity and self-esteem, and fostering intergenerational transmission of cultural knowledge and practices (Ford et al., 2014). Moreover, the importance of language revitalisation efforts in promoting linguistic justice for Indigenous communities should be emphasised by Australian government administrations. Such efforts could support various activities, including language documentation, language teaching and learning, and language policy development and implementation. By revitalising endangered languages and supporting language maintenance, Indigenous people can regain control over their linguistic and cultural resources and assert their rights to linguistic and cultural self-determination.

Government Policies to Support Indigenous Language and Culture

In recent years, the Australian Commonwealth Government has implemented policies to address the systemic oppression of Indigenous people and acknowledge their rights to maintain and revive their languages and cultures. For example, an agreement in 2019 that underpinned the pre-existing Federal Closing the Gap Policy, includes a commitment to supporting Indigenous and Torres Strait Islander languages, recognizing their importance in maintaining cultural identity, and promoting intergenerational healing (Department of the Prime Minister & Cabinet, 2019). The government has also launched the Indigenous Voice co-design process, which aims to enshrine Indigenous People’s voices in the Australian constitution and empower them to have a greater say in policy decisions that affect their communities (Department of the Prime Minister & Cabinet, 2021). Additionally, the Northern Territory government has launched a new program to fund bilingual education in remote communities, recognizing the benefits of incorporating Indigenous languages and cultures into the education system (Northern Territory Government, 2021). These policies demonstrate a growing recognition of the value of Indigenous languages and cultures and a commitment to promoting linguistic and cultural diversity in Australia. Conversely, the Department of Education Western Australia published data demonstrating that 10,000 people are learning Indigenous languages in schools, presenting it as a significant accomplishment. However, this number needs to be put into perspective as the population of Australia consists of 25.8 million people, 10,000 only accounts for 3.88% of the population. Although any progress in promoting and preserving Indigenous languages and cultures is important, the government's portrayal of this number as a significant achievement is misleading. It is crucial for the government to recognise the urgency and importance of revitalizing Indigenous languages and cultures and to take concrete steps to support and promote bilingualism and multilingualism. This includes not only providing resources and support for language education but also acknowledging and addressing the ongoing systemic barriers and historical injustices that have contributed to the loss and marginalization of Indigenous languages and cultures. Although there are emerging trends that uphold and support Indigenous Language instruction, the data shows that this is incipient and far too late for many. Indeed, a large majority of adult Australians never had the schooling opportunities to learn Indigenous languages, engage in truth-telling, or understand the impacts of colonisation.

The Future of Language Teaching in Australia: Towards Empowerment and Inclusivity

For many years, scholars in language teaching have advocated for incorporating Indigenous languages and cultures into language curricula (Ford et al., 2014; Hinton, 2017; Moreton-Robinson & Walter, 2009). They argue that this approach promotes linguistic justice and empowers Indigenous people to maintain and revive their languages and cultures. By acknowledging and incorporating the unique cultural and linguistic characteristics of Indigenous people, language learners can gain a deeper understanding of the complexity and diversity of Australian society. The examples provided of different approaches to incorporating Indigenous knowledge include using local Indigenous languages as the medium of instruction, integrating Indigenous content into language teaching materials, and involving Indigenous elders and knowledge holders in the teaching process. The latter point ensures that Indigenous knowledge is appropriately represented and respected.

Language learning encompasses more than linguistic skills acquisition, it is a matter of participating in social and cultural practices that connects people (Pavlenko & Lantolf, 2000). It should be viewed as a process of identity construction and participation. In the context of Indigenous languages, this means providing opportunities for learners to participate in meaningful activities that involve the use of language in authentic contexts. This approach not only promotes language learning but also helps learners to develop a deeper understanding of the complexity and diversity of Australian society. Therefore, language teaching should not only focus on teaching the language itself but also on promoting cultural understanding and respect.

One way for Australian governments to incorporate Indigenous languages and cultures into language teaching curricula is through the institution and use of bilingual education. Bilingual education involves teaching students in both their native language and the language of the majority culture. This approach has been shown to be effective in promoting academic success and preserving the cultural identities of minority language groups (Hinton, 2017). In the context of Indigenous languages, as there are over 250 Indigenous languages (including 800 dialects), this may initially seem to be a challenging task. However, governments could implement the languages of a particular Nation, correlated to its region to teach in schools in various states. This would also benefit Indigenous people in a socio-economic manner. Community elders could be a part of the teacher training programs passing their knowledge down to future generations. This could help to prevent the loss of these languages and promote their use and maintenance. Another approach to incorporating Indigenous languages and cultures into language teaching is through the use of culturally responsive pedagogy. Culturally responsive pedagogy involves using teaching methods that are responsive to the cultural backgrounds and experiences of individual students, at a classroom level (Hinton, 2017). This approach recognises the importance of culture and identity in the learning process and seeks to promote a more inclusive and equitable learning environment.

Australian educators and policymakers should recognise the value of bilingualism and take steps to promote the use and preservation of Indigenous languages alongside English. Initiatives that incorporate Indigenous knowledge in language teaching and learning have shown promise in revitalizing these languages (Hinton, 2017). Similar efforts have been undertaken in Bangladesh, where the public debates over the language of instruction have been examined in the print media (Hamid & Jahan, 2015). These initiatives demonstrate the potential of bilingual education in promoting language diversity, preserving cultural heritage, and achieving social justice. Therefore, incorporating Indigenous perspectives and knowledge into pre-service teacher education programs, providing professional development opportunities for practicing teachers, and developing resources and materials that reflect Indigenous cultures and histories are some effective ways to train teachers to participate in closing the gap. It is also crucial to engage Indigenous educators and community members in the development and delivery of these programs to ensure cultural relevance and authenticity.

Conclusion

The promotion of linguistic justice for Indigenous people in Australia is a crucial issue that requires attention from scholars and educators alike. The politics of discourse and language teaching play a significant role in perpetuating or challenging linguistic oppression and marginalisation. This paper has argued that it is crucial to promote language diversity and preserve Indigenous languages to ensure the recognition and value of Indigenous voices and identities. The loss of Indigenous languages has resulted in a loss of culture, knowledge, history, and identity for Indigenous communities, leading to disparities in various aspects of life. Incorporating Indigenous knowledge in language education and promoting bilingualism has shown promise in revitalising these languages and promoting cross-cultural understanding, and actual steps that contribute to closing the gap. The implications of promoting and preserving Indigenous languages are significant and training teachers to incorporate Indigenous knowledge and perspectives into language curricula plays a vital role in (re)shaping identities. Therefore, initiatives that promote language diversity, particularly Indigenous diversity, must continue to be supported in Australia and worldwide. This essay has analysed and presented issues faced by Indigenous Australians and has examined the potential for language teaching to be a powerful force for promoting Indigenous empowerment and advancing linguistic justice in Australia. Language teachers can support Indigenous students in reclaiming their voices and asserting their identities in a society that has historically silenced them. By engaging in ongoing dialogue and reflection, Australia can work towards a more just and equitable language teaching practice.
