Towards Linguistic Justice: The Politics of Discourse and Language Teaching for Aboriginal Empowerment in Australia

Abstract

The silencing of Aboriginal voices has been a persistent feature of Australian society since colonisation. Using critical discourse analysis, this essay argues that language teaching can be a powerful force for Aboriginal empowerment - by centring Aboriginal languages and cultures and challenging dominant monolingual norms.

Intro

The silencing of Aboriginal voices has been a persistent feature of Australian society since the legacy of colonization, with a long history of denying Aboriginal people their cultural practices, beliefs, and languages (Rose, 1991). The impact of this silencing has been profound, with Aboriginal people experiencing high levels of social and economic disadvantage, as well as ongoing trauma and intergenerational trauma (Anderson et al., 2016). This topic is highly relevant and related to language teaching because it highlights the importance of being aware of the power dynamics and their effects on language use in social relations. It is essential for language teachers to recognize the social and cultural context in which language is used, and to create a classroom environment that is inclusive and empowering for all students. Creating awareness of the way language is used to silence certain groups and perspectives, language teachers can encourage critical thinking and facilitate discussions that challenge dominant narratives and power structures (Pavlenko and Lantolf, 2000). Participating in a new social and cultural world, which involves the (re)construction of oneself and identity. This idea can be relevant to the development of intercultural competence, as understanding and navigating different cultural identities and perspectives is a key component of effective intercultural communication. This refers to the ability to communicate and interact effectively with people from diverse cultural backgrounds in general. Thus, incorporating Aboriginal languages and cultures in language teaching, students can develop a deeper understanding of Aboriginal perspectives and worldviews. This can enhance their intercultural competence and enable them to communicate and interact effectively with Aboriginal people. Furthermore, incorporating Aboriginal languages and cultures in language teaching can also challenge and disrupt the dominant monolingual and monocultural norms in Australian society. It can promote a more inclusive and diverse approach to language teaching and learning, which can benefit not only Aboriginal students but also non-Aboriginal students who may come from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds (May and Hill, 2008). Similarly, Hinton and Hale (2001) found that teaching the Navajo language and culture in schools in the United States helped to promote intercultural competence and understanding among students. Using a sociocultural lens allows us to understand how language use and discourse are intertwined with broader social structures and relationships (Miller, 1999). Sociocultural theory suggests that learning and development are shaped by social and cultural contexts, and that language use is a key means through which social practices and identities are constructed and negotiated (Vygotsky, 1978). In the case of Aboriginal people in Australia, language use has been the main struggle for cultural recognition and empowerment (Butt et al., 2013). This can promote language learning as a means of promoting social justice and empowering marginalized communities. For example, by encouraging students to learn and use languages that are traditionally marginalized or stigmatized, such as Aboriginal languages in Australia, language teachers can help promote linguistic diversity and challenge linguistic imperialism. This essay argues that language teaching has the potential to be a powerful force for promoting Aboriginal empowerment and advancing linguistic justice in Australia. By adopting a critical discourse analysis approach and centering Aboriginal languages and cultures, language teachers can support Aboriginal students in reclaiming their voices and asserting their identities in a society that has historically silenced them. By engaging in ongoing dialogue and reflection, we can work towards a more just and equitable language teaching practice that supports Aboriginal empowerment and advances linguistic justice.

Aboriginal Australians have been subjected to linguistic and cultural oppression throughout Australia's history, with their languages and cultures being suppressed in various ways, this is a well-established fact in the literature on Indigenous Australian languages and cultures (May and Hill, 2008). Since the colonization of Australia, Indigenous people were subjected to various forms of oppression, including linguistic and cultural oppression. The policies of assimilation and forced removal of children from their families, known as the Stolen Generations, had a significant impact on the transmission and maintenance of Indigenous languages and cultures (Henderson, 2011; Moreton-Robinson, 2000). As a result of these policies, many Indigenous people have experienced a loss of connection to their language and culture, leading to a loss of identity and self-esteem (Thieberger, 1997). The concept of linguistic justice arises from the recognition that language is a fundamental aspect of identity and culture, and the denial of the right to use one's own language can have significant negative impacts on an individual or group's well-being (Henderson, 2011). Linguistic justice advocates for the right of individuals or groups to use and maintain their own language and culture without being discriminated against or marginalized.

In recent years, scholars have shown a growing interest in promoting linguistic justice for Aboriginal people in Australia. It has been emphasized that recognizing Aboriginal languages and cultures as integral parts of Australia's heritage and identity is crucial for achieving linguistic justice (Morgan, 2017; O'Reilly, 2017). As argued by O'Reilly (2017), language is not just a means of communication but also a crucial element of identity and culture. By recognizing and supporting the use of Aboriginal languages, individuals can reclaim their heritage, strengthen their sense of identity and self-esteem, and foster intergenerational transmission of cultural knowledge and practices (Morgan, 2017).

Moreover, scholars have emphasized the importance of language revitalization efforts in promoting linguistic justice for Aboriginal communities (Hinton, 2001; Walsh, 2018). Such efforts involve various activities, including language documentation, language teaching and learning, and language policy development and implementation (Walsh, 2018). By revitalizing endangered languages and supporting language maintenance, Aboriginal people can regain control over their linguistic and cultural resources and assert their rights to linguistic and cultural self-determination (Hinton, 2001).

In this paper, Boroditsky investigates how different languages represent time and how these representations may influence cognitive processes. She examines two Aboriginal languages, Kuuk Thaayorre and Pormpuraaw, which use cardinal directions (north, south, east, west) rather than relative concepts (left, right, front, back) to describe spatial relationships.

Boroditsky's research suggests that the use of cardinal directions in these languages may lead to a heightened awareness and understanding of spatial relationships, as well as a different way of thinking about time. Specifically, speakers of these languages appear to have a greater ability to orient themselves in space and to remember past events in relation to their spatial context.

The paper has important implications for understanding the relationship between language and cognition, as well as the diversity of cognitive processes across different cultures and linguistic groups. While "How Languages Construct Time" does not specifically address the importance of teaching Aboriginal languages, it does provide evidence for the broader importance of language preservation and revitalization efforts.

Boroditsky's research highlights the ways in which language can shape our cognition and perception of the world around us. Different languages encode and emphasize different aspects of the environment and culture, which can in turn influence the way we think, reason, and interact with others.

For Indigenous peoples, language is often deeply intertwined with cultural identity and knowledge systems. The loss of language can have profound effects on individuals and communities, leading to a weakening or erasure of cultural traditions and ways of knowing.

Thus, the preservation and revitalization of Indigenous languages can play a crucial role in supporting cultural continuity and resilience. By promoting the use and teaching of Aboriginal languages, we can help to ensure that these languages and the unique worldviews and knowledge systems they embody are passed down to future generations.

In summary, while "How Languages Construct Time" does not directly address the importance of teaching Aboriginal languages, it does underscore the broader significance of language preservation efforts for cultural continuity and knowledge transmission.

Firstly, TESOL practitioners need to be aware of and sensitive to Indigenous education policy and the historical and cultural contexts of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students in Australia. Understanding the policy landscape and the challenges faced by Indigenous students in education can inform TESOL practitioners in developing inclusive and culturally responsive teaching practices. This includes recognizing the unique cultural identities and linguistic backgrounds of Indigenous students, acknowledging the impact of colonization and intergenerational trauma, and being aware of the policies and initiatives aimed at addressing the education gap between Indigenous and non-Indigenous students.

Secondly, TESOL practitioners need to recognize the importance of Indigenous representation in decision-making processes related to education. This includes advocating for the inclusion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander voices in policy development, curriculum design, and school decision-making. TESOL practitioners can support the empowerment of Indigenous communities by actively involving them in decision-making processes, fostering partnerships with local Indigenous communities, and valuing their input in shaping education policies and practices that impact Indigenous students' education.

Thirdly, TESOL practitioners need to consider the inclusion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories and cultures in their English language teaching. This can be achieved by incorporating Indigenous perspectives, literature, and cultural knowledge into the curriculum, lesson plans, and classroom activities. This can help promote a sense of pride and identity among Indigenous students, foster cross-cultural understanding, and contribute to the reconciliation process between Indigenous and non-Indigenous Australians.

Taken together, the topics of Indigenous education policy, Indigenous representation in decision-making, and the inclusion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories and cultures in education carry significant implications for TESOL practitioners. Being aware of these issues and integrating culturally responsive practices into their teaching allows TESOL practitioners to contribute to inclusive, respectful, and empowering educational environments for Indigenous students in Australia.
