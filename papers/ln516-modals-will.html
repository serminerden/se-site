Is ‘Will’ a Modal, or a Marker of Future Tense?

Abstract

A linguistics and grammar assessment in two parts: whether ‘will’ is a modal or a marker of future tense in English, and an analysis of modals and tenses in authentic text lines - from Reichenbach’s reference points to gradience in modality.

Section 1: Is ‘will’ a modal, or a marker of Future tense?

The English word ‘will’ is divergent in its use. This use has been debated by professionals for a long period of time. On the one hand, it is discussed that its use is a modal auxiliary with the aspect of future. As the future has not occurred yet, it therefore, remains uncertain. On the other hand, ‘will’ is seen as (a marker of) the future tense, and, that it can be used as a modal in small cases, depending on the mood that it is used with. This essay will investigate both sides of the argument to answer the question: Is ‘will’ a modal, or a marker of the Future tense?

The primary use of ‘will’ that everybody agrees upon is that, it is a verb. As Palmer (1987) points out, verbs are used with a subject to describe what someone or something does or what happens to them. There are two groups of verbs: auxiliary verbs and main verbs (as ‘will’ is an auxiliary verb, only that will be discussed). Auxiliary verbs are used with a main verb to form the predicate of a sentence. Auxiliary verbs can also be categorised in two groups: primary auxiliaries (to have, to be, to do) and modal auxiliaries also known as modals (can/could, shall/ should, will/would, may/might, must).

‘Will’ as a modal auxiliary is used in terms of obligation, certainty, possibility, and request. It is also known to be semantically strong, requiring facts of the situation (Huddleston, 1995). For example, She is coming tomorrow/ She will be coming tomorrow. The fact that she ‘will’ be coming tomorrow sounds stronger and more convincing than the first sentence. In most cases ‘will’ can be replaced by the modal ‘must’ as they are both seen as strong modals that represent epistemic modality, meaning, it makes a judgement of the truth and is the modality of knowledge and belief (Palmer, 2001). Alternatively, ‘will’ also represents dynamic modality which indicates ability and willingness in a more general sense (Palmer, 2001). As ‘will’ can show the willingness and ability of a person to do something, it can also be replaced by other modals. Therefore, ‘will’ is more polysemous in its use, compared to can/could and may/must (Coates, 1983). ‘Could’ and ‘may’ are seen as the polite version of ‘can’ and ‘must’, they work differently, whereas ‘will’ can function as ability, obligation, and request. See the examples below:

Can/ could/ will you open the door?

Where ‘can’ asks for the ability of a person, ‘could’ is requesting in a polite way to open the door. ‘Will’ on the other hand, is requesting a person if they are willing and able to open the door.

It may/ must/ will be his dirty socks.

Where ‘may’ points out uncertainty, ‘must’ is fully certain of who is the owner of the dirty socks. ‘Will’ is certain but acts more polite compared to ‘must’ and more certain compared to ‘may’.

These documents will be finished by tomorrow (.,!).

It can mean two things: certainty and prediction, that the documents will be finished and not to worry about it, it is certain; It is referring to the future it remains uncertain and how certain ‘will’ may sound, it is a prediction that the job will get finished by tomorrow.

In these examples (i, ii) ‘will’ is acting as a modal. In example (iii) ‘will’ is acting as a modal and the future tense.

As Comrie (1985) argues, we should not be misled by the name, ‘modal auxiliary’ as some of the modals do in fact act as a tense. Some of the modals are seen as markers of mood, while others are markers of future tense. Comrie gives the examples that it should be clear that only ‘will’ and ‘shall’ are the only modals that mark the future tense.

If only ‘will’ and ‘shall’ mark the future then the other modals should be considered before excluding them. For example:

Strong version: I will get you that flower tomorrow/ I can get you that flower tomorrow / I must get you that flower tomorrow / I shall get you that flower tomorrow. These show, willingness, intention, ability, and certainty.

Weaker version: I should get you that flower tomorrow / I could get you that flower tomorrow / I may get you that flower tomorrow / I might get you that flower tomorrow. These show, possibility and uncertainty.

Other capable verbs that mark the future in this example sentence:

I want to get you that flower tomorrow / I am going to get you that flower tomorrow. These show, willingness and intention.

All the examples given above have not happened yet, they may or may not occur. Their outcome is uncertain, and therefore, they should all be seen as markers of the future, according to most professionals. Nonetheless, example (ii) is more of a possibility and uncertain, which still might happen in the future. Context and pragmatics play a big part into understanding these sentences.

In countries where English is taught as a second language they learn that there are only two tenses; the past and the present. They refer to the future as: Talking about the future. Mainstream professionals will agree with that, and that the term future tense is traditionally reserved only for will + verb when it indicates future. All the other methods of talking about the past, the present, and the future are derived from the past and present tense. There are three ways of talking about the future (only the present tense form will be discussed. Some of the ideas are adapted from Capel and Sharp, 2002):

I will work. Will + the verb = the indication of the future.

I am going to work. To be going to + the verb = an intention or a plan.

I am to work. To be to + the verb = an arrangement by others or by destiny.

The use of (a) will +verb is preferred when there is an (i) indication of future time (not according to timetable), or when a (ii) condition is mentioned

I’ll be back in half an hour

If you eat healthier and exercise, you will get fit again.

The use of (b) to be going to + verb is preferred when (i) intention is expressed, or the (ii) expectation that something will happen.

I’m going to explore the city

We’re going to see change when Hilary Clinton becomes president.

Sometimes there are no differences between the present continuous and going to in some sentences. For example, They are getting married in June/ They are going to get married in June.

The use of (c) to be to + verb is used to express an (i) arrangement made by another person, or (ii) by destiny. To be to could also be an alternative to the modal MUST.

There is to be a general election

He is to change during his adolescence

The use of the present simple (timetabled future) and the present continuous (arranged future) are normally used to refer to the future by the mainstream linguists. Non-mainstream linguists, do not accept this method of usage. According to them the future tense is the only way to refer to the future. In my opinion, the use of the future tense in context would make teaching to second language learners easier, rather than ‘talking about the future’. It is not a clear term for them to understand the concept.

Salkie (2010, p 201) gives a perfect example of the use of ‘will’ in the past, where it behaves as the modal ‘may’: she may have already left/ she will have already left.

He also corrects the examples from Huddleston (2002) by pointing out the difference of epistemic ‘will’ and future ‘will’, instead of ‘prediction’ :

If they are here they’ll be upstairs (epistemic will)/ If they come they will sleep upstairs (future will).

Showing that ‘will’ functions as a modal and as an implication of the future depending on context.

In conclusion, considering all the information given, we can see the importance of ‘will’ as a modal. It can act as a strong obligation, give certainty and possibility, or act as a simple request. In all certainty it can be said that ‘will’ in all the scenarios given, makes all entailments sound stronger. In terms of ‘will’ as a marker of the future, I conclude, that with all the arguments provided, ‘will’ does act as a marker of the Future in most cases, as it refers to what has to come or what needs to happen after the words are uttered. In addition, it is not the only verb that marks the future as discussed previously. Context and pragmatics also play a large part indicating to something that needs to happen. It can also be argued that ‘will’ does not always indicate the event taking place in the Future as illustrated by Salkie (2010). All things considered, in answering the question posed, it is justifiable to say that ‘will’ is a modal and it marks the Future tense.

Section 2: Modals and Tenses

Line 1: Have been forced

This is the present perfect tense in the passive voice (Lester, 2012). The sentence changes from active to a passive form when, auxiliary of the perfect ‘to have’ and the auxiliary of the passive ‘to be’ are put together before the past participle of the main verb (Lester, 2012). It links the past to the present.

According to Reichenbach (1947), the present perfect refers to an event that takes place in the past. He simultaneously claims that there is a reference time in the past, which means that the present and past are linked in some way. Reichenbach’s ‘tens system’ (1947:77), reveals all complex tenses have a reference (R) point or time. For the present perfect tense, R and speech time (S) happen at the same time (S,R) which represent the present. The event time (E) the situation in question happens before R (E_R). The state of affairs at reference time is that the residence are no longer at the castle. Therefore, the event ‘have been forced’ happened before reference time (E_R). For the present perfect Reichenbach formulates: S,R+E_R. When the present perfect is replaced by the simple past:

Residents of a castle close to Balmoral forced to flee…….

This sentence makes the situation seem to be a completed action in the past, which it is not. It may have started in the past and the result is still clear at present time, as the people had to flee their houses and have not returned. This term is also known as the resultative (Lester, 2012).

Line 10: Had left

This is the past perfect tense (Lester, 2012). The past perfect is formed out of the auxiliary verb ‘to have’ in the past tense, followed by the past participle of the main verb (Lester, 2012). The past perfect shows that the actions have been done before another action which is in the past (Lester, 2012). This is also known as, the past within the past.

According to Reichenbach (1947), all complex tenses have a reference point or time (R), for the past perfect, R is always before speech time (S) and after event time (E). So there is a time of reference in the past that happened before the sentence was uttered (R_S) and the situation (event) in question happening before that reference time (E_R). For the past perfect Reichenbach formulates: R_S+E_R. Event time is ‘had left’ which happened before reference time ‘told’ which happened before speech time.

Line 10 also includes backshifting in indirect reported speech (Huddleston and Pullum, 2005). In indirect reported speech the reporter (in line 10) gives the exact meaning of Frazer’s remark, without using his exact words. Frazer’s utterance was probably ‘I have left’ which is in present perfect tense and it was reported as ‘he had left’ which is accurate, as the reporting word is ‘told’. This kind of change is referred to as backshifting (Huddleston and Pullum, 2005).

Line 38: Should

SHOULD is a modal that is seen as the weaker version of the modal MUST, and traditionally is a complex modal because, it can act as a tense (Groefsema, 1995).

SHOULD, can express epistemic modality, meaning, it makes a judgement of the truth and is the modality of knowledge and belief (Palmer, 2001). It is a fact that there is more bad weather coming, because of that knowledge, it is necessary that the government has to reconsider removing the direct support. In addition, it can also represent deontic modality, meaning, it is concerned with influencing actions. It is necessary for the government to reconsider their actions. (Palmer, 2001).

Coates (1983), would refer this to be ‘merger’, meaning it is unnecessary to decide which meaning is envisioned to understand this sentence. Merger is quite often found with SHOULD when it expresses a weak obligation.

According to Groefsema (1995), modals each have a single meaning with pragmatic interpretations, and she claims that the logical relation between propositions can be entailment or compatibility. There is a relationship between a modal and a propositional content, they are either compatible or entailment. Groefsema, classifies SHOULD as an entailment and that some pragmatic evidence (more bad weather is on its way) is needed to understand the content in this sentence.

Line 45: Can

CAN is a modal which represents ability, permission and possibility (Coates, 1983).

Palmer (2001), would consider this to be deontic permission and dynamic modality. Meaning, the words ‘the government is committed’ proximity of CAN, eliminates the options, necessity and possibility. However, it does show the government has permission (deontic), as there are rules and restrictions that the government has to follow, and therefore the willingness and ability (dynamic) to help, as they know the implications of the flooding are very serious.

Coates (1983) would use gradience to classify CAN from core of ‘ability’ to the periphery of ‘possibility’. However, she would find it difficult to judge and resolve the two meanings in the concept of “the fuzzy set” where Coates illustrates gradience from most used as the semantic ‘core’, least used as ‘periphery’, and the in-between as ‘skirt’ (Coates, 1983:14). In the end she would conclude, CAN to be ambiguous in her theoretical modal that is based around the theory of indeterminacy (Coates, 1983:16).

Papafragou (2000) controversially argues that, modals each have a single meaning with pragmatic interpretations, and claims that the logical relation between proposition and domain can be entailment or compatibility. In that case the proposition is compatible with the set of all propositions in domain (D) which is factual, meaning that it is of fact that the government knows what they can do and cannot do, and therefore, cannot be epistemic. It expresses deontic permission, if it is compatible with all the regulations that the government has to follow.

Line 48: Will

WILL is a complex modal that can also act as a tense to mark the future time (Groefsema, 1995).

According to Reichenbach (1947), this utterance would have been Future Tense, in which he would formulate that event time (E) in this sentence WILL will happen after speech time (S), after the sentence was uttered. However, the time marker in this sentence is the next financial year.

In line 48, WILL functions as a modal to express the certainty that the next financial year will include 4 million. When leaving WILL out of the sentence, it still functions without it, but its meaning becomes less certain. By replacing WILL with other modals ‘can/could/should/would’ it changes the meaning of the sentence completely by making the sentence weaker. It becomes a possibility of happening whereas, using WILL or MUST it becomes a certainty that it will happen.

Coates (1983) would classify this modal as being ambiguous by distinction of root-epistemic modality. Epistemic, meaning, it makes a judgement of the truth of the preposition-modality of knowledge and belief. The judgement of the truth and knowledge is that John Swinney (who is uttering the sentence) is saying, that he has the knowledge of the government’s spending plans, and ROOT (deontic ability) gives permission, or lays obligation (Coates, 1983). In this case the certainty and prediction of the government spending the money is there, as they have the permission to spend that money in the next financial year.
