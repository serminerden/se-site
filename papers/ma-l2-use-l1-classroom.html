The Effect of L2 Use in Language Teaching: The Role of L1 in the Classroom

Abstract

First language use in the second language classroom has been controversial for decades. This essay weighs the evidence on L2-only teaching versus principled L1 use across EFL and ESL settings, and what it means for classroom practice.

Introduction

First language (L1) use in second language (L2) classrooms has been a controversial topic for decades and has established its mark in the field of Applied Linguistics (Tsagari and Giannikas, 2020). Advocates of an L2-only approach argue that using the L1 in the classroom leads to inhibiting the language learning process, and sole exposure to the L2 leads to second language acquisition (SLA), resulting in proficient learners (Ellis, 2005; Gass& Mackey, 2002). This idea resonates with Krashen’s (1982) input hypothesis that exposure to comprehensible input subconsciously leads to language acquisition by focusing on meaning not form. The introduction of Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) revolutionised language classrooms in the way that learners moved away from the passive Grammar-Translation (GT) method and into the active CLT method where learners were given a voice (Kalaja and Barcelos, 2013; Snoder, 2019). The primary goal of CLT is for learners to obtain communicative competence in the L2 as it is meaningful and authentic to communicate with peers in the Target Language (TL) (Snoder, 2019). Katz and Snow (2009) illustrate that the way lessons are taught can differ in various countries (i.e., different standards, regulations, and policies with regard to teaching English). As there is a difference between learning English as a Foreign Language (EFL) and learning English as a Second Language (ESL): EFL is taught in countries where the L1 is different from the L2 (e.g., Japan, France, Brasil), which could cause learners and teachers to prefer using the L1 in the classroom. ESL is taught in countries where the preferred L2 is the native language (e.g., Australia, Canada, England), this could lead to opting for the L2 in the classroom as teachers may not be able to speak the L1s of their learners. Ellis (2005) and VanPatten (2020) state that the level of frequency in exposure to the TL leads to implicit learning, thus potentially leading to implicit knowledge, which is crucial in SLA, as it is similar to L1 acquisition. However, the use of L1 in L2 classrooms has received a lot of praise as it may create social and cognitive prospects through collaborative work with peers. Vygotsky’s (1978) sociocultural theory allows learners to use their L1 in the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), which creates a safe environment that reduces learners’ anxiety. It also promotes conducive learning as learners can easily convey meaning in the L2, making it easier for teachers to efficiently explain the context and build rapport with students (Ellis and Shintani, 2013). This essay will explain why the use of L2 in the classroom is necessary for learners to improve their language proficiency, and why the use of the L1 should be minimised or avoided.

The role of the L2 in the classroom

CLT changed language classrooms, allowing learners to step out of the submissive role of the GT method and into the interactive CLT role where the learners could immerse with peers in the L2 (Kalaja and Barcelos, 2013; Snoder, 2019). Cole (1998) argues that some scholars suggest that CLT does not have a stance on the use of L1 as it has minimal mention in the literature. Nevertheless, Ellis and Shintani (2013) counter this view by arguing that the use of the L1 is an issue in CLT classrooms as learners focus on finishing the task given using the L1, rather than trying to resolve the problems in the L2. The excitement when CLT was introduced to the world, came from allowing learners to immerse themselves in the L2 and reach communicative competence in the L2 as it is a meaningful and authentic way to communicate with peers in the TL (Snoder, 2019). In order to achieve language competence, learners need to be exposed to the L2 as the frequency of exposure is critical for language acquisition (Ellis, 2005; Gass& Mackey, 2002). It is important for learners to use their L2 knowledge because the way they attain their language reflects the way they use it (VanPatten, 2020). Showing a preference for only teaching or instructing in the L2 sets the example, gives room for error correction, and exposes learners to the TL. Frequent exposure to the TL leads to implicit learning which could lead to implicit knowledge and storage of attained knowledge in long-term memory (VanPatten, 2020).

Snoder (2019) argues that even though people in Sweden do not speak English as their L1, it still should be considered an ESL country because: Teaching and learning of English are solely done in the L2 in Scandinavian countries. People are exposed to English on a daily basis (e.g., television, music, advertising). The proficiency level of English is based on the consistent and sole use of L2. Vold (2022) makes the same argument as Snoder (2019) when conducting an empirical study in Norway where two classrooms were compared based on the number of TL turns between teachers and students. The L2 English classroom was considered ESL and the L3 French classroom EFL. After analysis, it became evident the students would solely use English in the ESL classes which made them aware of their errors. This output-prompting strategy would then push the learners to self-correct and speak the TL more proficiently. The students would show interest and contribute to the class in the L2 and show improvement. However, in the EFL classes, the students would use their L1 frequently. The main comments given by the learners were focussed on the use of L1 by the teacher and the fact that the EFL materials were not as interesting as those in the L2 classrooms, which caused a lack of motivation leading students to revert to the L1.

Similarly, Sato and Storch (2022) researched an EFL group in Chile and an ESL group in Australia. In order to get fair results, they discouraged the students from using the L1 in both countries, because the ESL group had mixed nationalities and the learners did not have the opportunity to use the L1. The results revealed that EFL learners engaged in self-initiated conversations and used L1 to resolve language problems in comparison wih ESL learners. However, what was interesting and contrasting with Vold’s research, was when students’ beliefs were measured in this study: the ESL learners found the use of L1 necessary compared to the EFL learners, showing student beliefs contradicted data (i.e., EFL learners did use their L1 and thought they did not need it).

Snoder’s (2019) study reveals: The importance of form-focused instruction in the L2 to raise metalinguistic awareness. Learners found purposely studying collocations for a test, using a glossary, the most effective input processing procedure for enabling L2 learning (L2L1) A dictogloss with a semantic elaboration to make a comparison to the L1 (L2L1). Learners were able to learn and understand the meaning of the TL in the L2 and then link it to their L1, not the other way around. In other words, the researcher emphasises the lack of language attainment when the TL is in the L1 which then is translated to the L2, as the TL can be learned wrong. This suggests that language teaching and instruction should be in the L2.

The role of L1 in the classroom

The use of L1 in L2 classrooms has received a lot of praise as it may create social and cognitive prospects through collaborative work with peers. Sociocultural theory allows learners to use their L1, which creates a safe environment as learners start their language learning journey by translating their L1 into the L2 (VanPatten, 2020). This allows learners to compare the L2 to the pre-existing knowledge of their L1. Allowing the use of L1 in classrooms reduces learner anxiety and promotes conducive language learning as learners can easily convey meaning in the L2, which makes it easier for teachers to explain the context of the TL and build rapport with students (Ellis and Shintani, 2013). However, according to a study conducted in a beginners’ class in Mexico, when the teacher did not use the L2 in the classroom, the learners felt that it removed the opportunity for exposure to the TL (Brook-Lewis, 2009). Even though using the L2 may lead to learner anxiety, it also brings an element of boosted confidence and encouragement when the message is understood in the L2. This confidence could lead to motivation in learning, and the production of the L2 instead of codeswitching back to the safe zone of the L1 (Ellis and Shintani, 2013). Codeswitching may be seen as an effective form of communication in the classroom, but it permits learners to speak in their L1 whenever it is convenient rather than how to resolve the issue in the L2 (Almohaimeed and Almurshed, 2018). Almohaimeed and Almurshed (2018) conducted an empirical study, measuring student beliefs towards L1 in L2 classrooms at Saudi University, in response to the positive outlook on the role of L1 in classrooms in other Arabic-speaking countries. The participants were learning English in preparation for their university studies. The results were compelling as the lower-level students were unwilling to use the L1 in the L2 classroom, as they thought it would hinder their learning. However, they only accepted the use of L1 by the teacher to explain complex vocabulary and grammar, as the learners believed that this would not hinder their learning. On the contrary, the intermediate and advanced learners, strongly believed that the L1 should be avoided by the learners and teachers as they were capable of negotiating for meaning in the L2. This type of collaborative work leads to motivation and enforces risk-taking, whereas the use of the L1 would impede their learning experience. These results may have heavily relied on the fact that these students were highly motivated to study at the university.

Another argument to put forward is that the studies presented in this essay were all conducted in different countries, with different types of learners, and different motivation levels, which resulted in different conclusions. Different countries have different standards when it comes to language teaching and learning (Katz and Snow, 2009). Individual differences in SLA learning could result in different types of learners and different outcomes when it comes to success in learning a language. Another contributing factor to varying results from studies is the subjectivity of participants’ beliefs. Borg (2009) argues that beliefs are derived from perceptions, personal theories or experiences, and cultural influences, as beliefs have been researched in many fields (i.e., anthropology, psychology, and education). This theory can easily make one think that beliefs influence learners’ choice of strategies, motivation, anxieties, and self-perception. Over time, individuals’ opinions are built based on these beliefs, thus, they are aware of their opinions and talk about these concepts of beliefs on a daily basis (Kalaja and Barcelos, 2013). These opinions and beliefs carry the individual’s version of what is right or wrong, however, this does not mean that those ideas are the truth. This makes it hard to pinpoint the concept of beliefs as each individual carries different beliefs, which makes it impossible to measure and state these results as facts. However, Snoder and Vold, both from two different Scandinavian countries, have put forward the influence of the frequency of exposure to the TL in solely L2 use by teachers and learners. They both consider teaching in their countries as ESL as their learners have high proficiency levels due to this concept, and their data does not rely on beliefs, but on test results, which renders it scientific.

Teaching Implications

Avoiding the use of L1 is inevitable but permitting the use of it leads to implications in L2 learning. The idea that using the L1 is an easy way to regulate a classroom only works if the teacher speaks the L1, which is impossible in many cases. If the teacher allows one student to use their phone to translate, the student’s focus would remain on translating everything they hear, or not paying enough attention to the class. This could have implications for the teacher in terms of: Poor classroom management: students speaking in various L1s or using their phones, and not listening to the teacher. Other students might feel excluded by learners who do not share the same L1: which removes autonomous learning and creates different social groups that divide collaborative learning within one classroom. It puts a strain on the teacher to speak multiple languages: which they are unlikely to achieve. If the teacher does speak another language: only the students who speak the same L1 would be able to get L1 assistance, which would not be fair to the rest of the class. It allows negative L1 transfer: confusion in the CLT classroom, particularly shy students who may copy errors from more dominant learners. For example, I had a discussion with my German and Spanish-speaking students who would revert back to their L1 when things were challenging in the classroom. I would ask them to stop translating from their L1 and use an English dictionary to understand the meaning in English, as some words do not translate well. For instance, both German and Spanish have grammatical gender in their languages, and some words have the opposite grammatical gender (e.g., a bridge is feminine in German and masculine in Spanish). This causes learners from those language backgrounds to use adjectives which are related to that gender rather the English forms (Boroditsky, Schmidt & Phillips, 2003). Both students started comparing the theory and agreed when finding a different example, i.e., both show interest in a girl, the Spanish student would say, you shine like the moon (the moon is feminine in Spanish), whereas the German student would say, you shine like the sun (the sun is feminine in German). This example allowed me to correct the error in English because I have pre-existing knowledge of learning both of those languages. However, it only had an impact on those students who spoke German and Spanish, but excluded the other students (i.e., Japanese, Turkish, Korean, Dutch) who do not have grammatical gender in their L1. Grammatical gender is only one example which does not translate well in English. The use of L2 will encourage learners to think in English and compare it to their L1 rather than translating everything from their L1, which improves the development of communicative competence (Snoder, 2019).

Conclusion

To sum up, although the sole use of L2 may not be achievable in all classrooms, it undoubtedly benefits students and their learning outcomes more than allowing the use of L1. Therefore, the use of the L2 should be maximised and the use of L1 minimised, preferably avoided. The use of L2 not only helps students to better apply skills learned in the classroom out in the real world, but it also increases the transfer of their implicit and explicit learning into implicit and explicit knowledge. Thus, based on the evidence presented in this essay, teachers should incorporate L2 use and instruction in class to assist students to become proficient in developing and using their L2 language skills.
