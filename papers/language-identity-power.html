Language, Identity and Power

Abstract

A reflection on how language, identity and power intertwine: how we use language, by whom it is used, and why - including how people identify themselves within a language, and the linguistic tactics through which politicians and institutions gain and exercise power.

Statement of Relevance

This module has dissected Language, Identity, and Power in order to enable me to view and interpret language from another angle. Language is a complex entity to consider and consequently I was challenged during this module to put the new knowledge gained into practise by presenting two specific topics given during the lectures. The first presentation was related to ethnicity and its impact on identity, and the second presentation focused on language and the way technology might influence how language is evolving.

This module has made me think about how we use language, by whom it is used, and why. It has enabled me to identify and describe differences in language use, how people can identify themselves within a language as well as the different ways in which language is linked to concerns of identity and power (van Herk, 2012). It was interesting to learn how politicians gain power by using certain linguistic tactics to manipulate their audiences. In addition, it was interesting to note the differences in tactics used by male and female politicians. However, power does not have to relate to politics, it can also be used on a personal level. This module was an inspiration in terms of understanding how language can shape your identity as an individual and how the variety of language use, for example “the New York accent” can be used in the United States (as New Yorkers are known to be straightforward and honest), in the form of power to reach your personal goals at work.

First Presentation: Ethnicity and Identity

Due to the different ethnic backgrounds within the group it was an obvious decision to select the topic of ethnicity for the first presentation; the relevance of this topic made it an obvious choice. Furthermore, this gave the group the ability to make the presentation more authentic and augmented due to the different nationalities. By providing examples in our presentation, we were able to show the audience how we engaged with our topic. In order to understand the comparisons that were made in the article given, it was necessary to conduct in-depth research in order to understand the characters in the TV-shows that were mentioned. The key elements were the relevant video-clips to illustrate the differences in range and frequency of African American English (AAE) use by Buckwild (Fix, 2010). Knowledge gained from Labov’s theory increased awareness on how we revert to our natural state when we get emotional. This is the best way we know how to express ourselves in moments of rational reasoning, and this was evident during the video-clips provided. In my opinion, Fix (2010) argued the obvious: that media speakers exceed their AAE use and make community speakers look inauthentic. Fix (2010) also implies we should not be judgemental as television creates certain stereotypes, because of that, community speakers sound and appear inauthentic.

The information gathered during the investigation and research was detailed, accurate, relevant, and well researched due to knowledge gained on how to prepare a presentation in the first week of the module (Chivers, Shoolbred, 2007). The feedback on the first presentation was positive, with the suggestion to give the audience more time to respond to questions.

Second Presentation: Is Texting Killing the English Language?

For the second presentation, we selected the topic ‘Is texting killing the English language?’. This topic was chosen for a variety of reasons. Firstly, it has no resemblance to the initial presentation. Secondly, we wanted to challenge ourselves and give our audience an even better performance. Above all, this is a topic that involves everyone who owns a mobile phone. After comparing different articles we critically selected the three most relevant ones to use for the second presentation: How teenagers create discourse markers and introduce them in terms of ‘DigiTalk’ (Turner, Abrams, Katic and Donovan, 2014); How teenagers outgrow abbreviations as they get older (Tagliamonte and Denis, 2008); How teenagers are innovating and enriching the language by texting (McWhorter, 2013). This research led us to argue broadly that texting is not, in fact, killing the language. The articles used gave us a clearer understanding of the topic. It made structuring our visual aids easier and in a logical order to make sure the audience could follow with interest and understanding, rather than simply reading the contents of the presentation. In contrast to our first presentation, this one was enhanced, more critical and sophisticated, because we had the freedom to select our own articles. The TED Talk used encouraged me to read McWhorter’s book ‘What Language Is’. Furthermore, we took the topic to another level by questioning our interaction skills with one another, texting might not be ‘killing’ the language but it certainly is affecting our interaction skills. It made us aware of our own behaviour while texting. Incorporating previous feedback, we asked the class to consider the question whilst we gave a small explanation about the topic. As a result, the students were more than willing to give a response.

Looking Ahead: Future Research

From our findings in our last research and the topics presented in the module, I would be interested in researching interaction skills. It is of relevance to me, because it affects all of us. My preference would be to compare decades beginning in the 1980s, because that was a time where we had no access to internet, and mobile phones were in their infancy. Interesting subjects to compare would be interaction, discourse, ethnography of communication, speech situations, speech events, speech acts, conversation analysis and communicative competence. Moreover, I would like to concentrate on the possibility of restitution of connectivity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the lectures were well structured to guide us through the seminars by giving us knowledge and inspiration on all the topics that were presented. The first presentation has made me see ethnicity from a different angle. It has helped me understand that it is not only ethnicity that shapes a part of your identity, it is also a group membership while growing up, or a group you want to belong to as an individual. The second presentation has given me the insight that technology is enhancing the evolution of language, whilst simultaneously “killing” our interaction skills. All things considered, it is reasonable to say that I have developed a better understanding of the interactions and interconnectedness of Language, Identity, and Power.
