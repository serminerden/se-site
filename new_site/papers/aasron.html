Meaning, Logic, and Quantification

Abstract

This paper explores how humans construct, interpret, and communicate meaning, bridging the domains of semantics, logic, and quantification. Part 1 introduces the building blocks of language, examining how words, syntax, and context combine to create meaning, and distinguishing between literal and associative interpretations. Part 2 analyses the conditional "if" and its representation in formal logic, highlighting the differences between material, indicative, and counterfactual conditionals, as well as the role of pragmatics in mediating understanding. Part 3 investigates universal and existential quantifiers, comparing their formal representation in predicate logic with the richness and flexibility of natural language, while addressing issues of scope, negation, and ambiguity. Throughout the paper, the emphasis is on the dynamic nature of meaning, highlighting how it evolves in relation to context, culture, and time. By integrating linguistic theory, logical structures, and philosophical reflection, the work offers a unified framework for understanding human communication.

Introduction

Communication is the process of transmitting information from one person to another (Keyton, 2007). Humans construct meaning through words, sentences, and context, yet understanding how this happens requires careful attention to the rules, patterns, and reasoning embedded in language. This paper explores three interconnected domains: semantics (how words and sentences carry meaning), logic (how humans reason about possibilities and consequences), and quantifiers (how humans generalise or specify). Each section builds upon the previous one, revealing how humans create, interpret, and negotiate meaning in a complex, dynamic world.

Part 1 – How People Mean

Communication is the process of transmitting information from one being to another (Keyton, 2007). Information itself is a pattern of data organised in a particular way. A sentence, for instance, consists of symbols that form words in a specific language, each carrying particular meanings.

Even the way words sound can alter a message. The phonotactics of a language refer to the permitted arrangements of sounds (i.e., phonemes) in that language (Yule, 2013). The way humans construct, understand, and share meaning is known as semantics (Löbner, 2012).

One essential distinction in semantics is between conceptual meaning and associative meaning. Conceptual meaning is the literal, dictionary definition of a word or sentence. Associative meaning refers to the personal and cultural associations that accompany a word throughout a person's life.

These connections form what is known as lexical meaning, the network of related words and concepts that the human mind draws upon to comprehend language (Kearns, 2011). Humans organise these relationships in categories, such as:

  • Synonymy: words with similar meanings (e.g., freedom/liberty)
  • Antonymy: words with opposite meanings (e.g., slow/fast)
  • Hyponymy: hierarchical relationships between words (e.g., animal/dog, dog/poodle) (Löbner, 2012).

However, meaning also depends on the structure of a sentence. Structural meaning is derived from syntax (i.e., the arrangement of words). All English sentences follow certain grammatical patterns, or rules, that dictate how words combine to form meaning.

Differences in interpretation can be subtle or drastic, depending on culture, language, age, education, and experience. The human ability to recognise what is meant without it being explicitly stated is called pragmatics (Kearns, 2011).

Part 2 – If → Conditionals in Logic and Language

In English, the word if expresses that something may or may not happen, depending on certain conditions. This is why its technical name is the conditional. This section explores the linguistic and logical dimensions of if.

When someone speaks truthfully, that determines the truth-value of a sentence. Negation can reverse this value: if the original proposition is true, its negation is false, and vice versa. A proposition is the part of a sentence or utterance that holds a truth value, true (T) or false (F).

Logic, the science of reasoning, examines what makes an argument valid (Gamut, 1991). Logic can be divided into informal logic (the study of arguments in natural language) and formal logic (the analysis of validity through symbols and structure).

Propositions are often symbolised by letters, commonly P and Q. Logical connectives link propositions together. The most common symbol used for the conditional is "→" and is often read as:

if → then ≡ P implies Q ≡ if P, then Q ≡ P only if Q ≡ P → Q.

For example: If I stand in the rain, then I will get wet.

Here, P is the antecedent (the sufficient condition for Q), and Q is the consequent (the necessary condition for P).

Modus Ponens states: if P → Q and P is true, then Q must also be true.

Modus Tollens states: if P → Q and Q is false, then P must also be false.

To summarize, the material conditional concerns only the truth-values of its parts, represented by the single-headed arrow. The indicative conditional reflects the real relationship between antecedent and consequent, while counterfactuals express hypothetical or speculative conditions.

Part 3 – How Adequately Do the Universal and Existential Quantifiers Model the Facts of Quantification in Natural Languages?

In order to answer the question posed it is of importance to understand how logic came to life, how restricted propositional logic is compared predicate logic, and to show the strengths and weaknesses of the quantifiers used in predicate logic and natural language.

Predicate logic is a rationalised version of a language of thought that was introduced by the German philosopher and mathematician Gottlob Frege, and the American philosopher, logician, mathematician, and scientist Charles Sanders Peirce.

There are two symbols in predicate logic that is supposed to represent all quantifiers in natural language, the universal quantifier (∀x) which carries the meaning: for all objects (x) in the domain of discourse each arbitrary called 'x' has a certain property. The existential quantifier (∃x): there exists an x in the domain of discourse arbitrarily called x such that x a certain property.

The universe of discourse is the set of all things humans wish to talk about; the set of all objects that can be sensibly assigned to a variable in a propositional function.

However, the scopes of quantifiers are limited to the antecedent and the consequent, respectively, so the variable x can be recycled.

The collaboration between quantification and negation in predicate logic:

  • ¬∀x(Ax → Bx) is equivalent to ∃x¬(Ax → Bx), which is in turn equivalent to ∃x(Ax & ¬Bx),
  • ∀x(Ax → ¬Bx) is equivalent to ¬∃x¬(Ax →¬Bx), which is in turn equivalent to ¬∃x(Ax & Bx).

Conclusion

On the one hand, natural languages use quantifiers the same way as the universal and existential quantifiers when quantifying without numerals, they remain unspecified. On the other hand, predicate logic has only two symbols to quantify all the different types of unspecified quantifiers in natural languages, which shows one of its limitations.

Where Frege, Pierce, and other scientists have complemented and renovated propositional logic into predicate logic, yet, it seems that the universal and existential quantifiers model the facts of quantification in natural languages to a certain level.
